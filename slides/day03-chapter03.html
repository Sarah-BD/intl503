Why Trade Cooperation Is Hard

Does the World Still Need the WTO?

Two Trends Since Doha Launched in 2001

Multilateral negotiations have completed zero new rounds. Regional agreements have not stopped.

Open by putting two numbers side by side. Regional trade agreements in force have risen from 102 in 1998 — three years before Doha launched — to 383 today, a rise that continued straight through the entire Doha-stall period. In that same twenty-five years, the multilateral track completed zero new rounds; the last one to finish was the Uruguay Round, back in 1994. Name the tension directly: the venue built to negotiate trade rules multilaterally has stalled, while governments keep making deals outside it. Yet the textbook claim — and most analysts still agree — is that governments still need the WTO, because cooperation on trade is genuinely hard and there’s no substitute institution. Preview today’s three-step argument: gains from trade, the cooperation problem, the WTO’s solution. Move briskly — five minutes for this section. The point is to plant the puzzle, not solve it.

The Economic Case for Trade

Comparative Advantage

Every country gains from trade by specializing in what it produces relatively well, even if it produces everything at higher cost than its trading partner.

What is not required

  • An absolute cost advantage in any good
  • The United States gains from trading with China even if U.S. firms could produce every good more cheaply

What is required

  • Different opportunity costs across the same goods
  • Each country specializes in the good for which it gives up less of others
  • Both countries consume beyond what autarky allowed

This is the Core Idea slide. State the principle plainly before any geometry. Most students have heard “comparative advantage” but very few can articulate it correctly. The distinguishing claim is that absolute advantage is not required — even a country that’s worse at producing everything still gains from trade, because what matters is the relative costs across goods within each country. Set this up clearly here and the rest of the section follows. If students leave today with one thing about trade theory, this is it.

The Production Possibility Frontier

The PPF shows everything a country could produce, given its labor and capital.

Its slope is the opportunity cost: how much of one good must be given up to make one more of the other.

Anchor the comparative advantage discussion in the PPF. Walk through the U.S. example from Oatley: if the U.S. throws everything at computers, it can make 100 million. If it throws everything at shirts, it can make 300 million. The line connecting those points is the PPF — every combination on the line is achievable. The slope is the opportunity cost: to make one more computer, the U.S. has to give up 3 shirts. We’re using constant opportunity costs (a straight line) to keep things simple; Oatley notes most contemporary models use diminishing returns (a curve), but the political-economy logic is the same either way. Don’t dwell on the geometry — students will see it again with China and then with both countries together. Just make sure they grasp that the line shows a trade-off, not a list of choices.

China Has a Different Frontier

Same logic, different opportunity costs — because China has different factor endowments (more labor, less capital).

China’s PPF is much steeper. China can make 400 million shirts if it specializes, but only 20 million computers — a function of its factor endowments (abundant labor, less capital). The opportunity cost of a computer in China is 20 shirts, compared to 3 in the U.S. Note: I am using Oatley’s numbers exactly — these are illustrative, not empirical. The pedagogical point is that the two countries have different opportunity costs, not the specific numbers. Different opportunity costs are what make trade beneficial. If both countries had identical PPFs, there would be no gains from trade — no specialization possible. The geometry of comparative advantage hinges on the gap between the two slopes (3 vs. 20). That gap is where the gains come from.

Trade Lets Both Consume Beyond Autarky

The two countries agree on a trade price (Oatley uses 6 shirts per computer).

Each fully specializes. Both consume on a higher indifference curve than autarky allowed.

Reproduces Oatley’s Figure 3.3 in spirit — full specialization with trade. Walk through the numbers: U.S. specializes in computers (produces 100 million), exports most of them, gets shirts in return. China specializes in shirts (produces 400 million), exports most, gets computers in return. At the trade price of 6 shirts per computer, U.S. consumption moves from (60 computers, 120 shirts) under autarky to roughly (75, 150) with trade. China’s consumption moves from (13, 140) to (25, 250). Both countries are unambiguously better off — they consume more of both goods than they could produce on their own. That’s the welfare engine that makes the rest of the chapter (and the rest of the field) make sense. If trade weren’t mutually beneficial, the cooperation problem wouldn’t matter. Because it is, the cooperation problem becomes a puzzle worth solving — which is what the WTO does.

Where Does Comparative Advantage Come From?

Ricardian model (Ricardo, 1817)

Comparative advantage comes from differences in labor productivity — i.e., technology.

Countries specialize where their technology gives them an edge.

Key insight: technology, not endowments, drives the pattern.

Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) model (early 20th c.)

Comparative advantage comes from factor endowments — the relative supply of capital, labor, land.

  • Capital-abundant countries → capital-intensive goods (autos, aircraft)
  • Labor-abundant countries → labor-intensive goods (apparel)

Key insight: who owns the abundant factor wins from trade.

Two workhorse models. Ricardo (early 19th c.) said comparative advantage comes from technology — Britain has better looms, so Britain makes cloth more productively than Portugal even if both have the same labor. Heckscher-Ohlin (early 20th c.) said it comes from factor endowments — Britain has more capital, so Britain specializes in capital-intensive goods, while Portugal with more labor specializes in labor-intensive goods. The bar chart below illustrates the H-O intuition with Oatley’s example: in auto manufacturing, labor is only about 25–30% of costs, with capital dominating; in apparel, labor takes the lion’s share. The H-O punchline is the one that matters most for political economy: the owners of the abundant factor gain from trade, while the owners of the scarce factor lose. In the U.S., that means capital-abundant industries (and capital owners) gain from trade; labor-abundant ones (and lower-skilled workers) lose. Hold onto that thought — it’s exactly the kind of distributional asymmetry that drives the politics in Chapter 4. We don’t need to do the full Stolper-Samuelson theorem today, but the intuition is: trade isn’t neutral within a country, even when it’s net-positive overall.

Trade Policy and Welfare

Standard introductory microeconomics, not in Oatley. The left panel shows the welfare consequences of opening a closed economy to trade; the right panel shows how a tariff partially reverses them.

Opening to trade transfers area A from producers to consumers and creates area B as new welfare. A tariff partially reverses this: area A is transferred back to producers as new producer surplus, area C is collected by the government, and areas B and D are destroyed as deadweight loss.

This slide isn’t in Oatley but it’s the strongest economic argument for trade. Set up the small open economy: world price is below domestic autarky price; the country imports the difference. A tariff raises the domestic price above the world price. Producers respond by producing more (Q1 → Q2). Consumers respond by buying less (Q4 → Q3). Imports shrink. Government collects tariff revenue on what’s still imported (rectangle C). Now the welfare accounting: producers gain area A (their surplus increases because they get a higher price). Government collects area C (tariff revenue). But consumers lose A + B + C + D (their surplus falls by a much larger trapezoid). Net welfare change: gains are A + C; losses are A + B + C + D; net is −B − D. Areas B and D are pure deadweight loss — production we didn’t need at this cost, and consumption that doesn’t happen because the price is artificially high. This is the punchline: protection doesn’t transfer welfare from consumers to producers. It transfers some welfare to producers and government, AND destroys some welfare entirely. That’s why “free trade is good” isn’t just an ideological claim — it’s a welfare arithmetic claim. If we go back to the cooperation problem from earlier, this is what’s at stake: when governments fail to cooperate, they leave B and D on the table. The cost of WTO failure isn’t abstract; it’s measurable in lost welfare.

What This Theory Assumes

The standard comparative-advantage argument rests on three contested assumptions:

Market clearing

Prices and wages adjust freely; labor markets clear; no persistent unemployment.

Trade adjustment costs in practice can persist for decades and remain geographically concentrated.

Constant or decreasing returns

Markets are competitive rather than oligopolistic.

Trade in aircraft, semiconductors, and software is dominated by a small number of firms operating under increasing returns to scale.

Neutral terms of trade

Relative prices do not systematically favor some producers over others.

The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis argues that commodity exporters face deteriorating terms of trade against manufactured-goods exporters.

We will return to each of these assumptions when we cover development models and trade strategy.

This is a signposting slide — flagging that the theory we just walked through makes assumptions that aren’t always true. Three big ones. (1) Market clearing: standard trade theory assumes prices and wages adjust to keep labor employed. In reality, the China Shock literature (Autor-Dorn-Hanson and others) shows that trade adjustment can be slow and geographically concentrated, with displaced workers not finding equivalent employment for years or decades. (2) Constant or decreasing returns to scale: the textbook story assumes competitive markets — many firms, no one with market power. Modern trade in aircraft, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, software is dominated by a small number of firms with massive economies of scale. That’s “new trade theory” (Krugman and others) — strategic interaction, market power, government intervention can shape who wins. Different politics, different policy implications. (3) Neutral terms of trade: the model assumes the relative prices of traded goods don’t systematically favor some producers. Prebisch and Singer argued in the 1950s that commodity exporters (developing countries) face deteriorating terms of trade against manufactured-goods exporters — a key intellectual foundation of import-substitution strategies in Latin America and elsewhere. We don’t have time to do these critiques justice today. Just flag them so students aren’t shocked when they show up later. We’ll revisit when we cover development.

Distribution Within Countries

Distributional conflict over trade is both interstate and intrastate. Country-level accounts of “winners” and “losers” obscure the within-country coalitions that produce national trade positions.

Between countries

Some countries gain more from a given agreement than others. Bargaining power determines the distribution (Section 3).

Within countries

Industries, regions, and factor owners gain and lose unevenly.

U.S. agriculture is heavily protected and subsidized despite farmers comprising roughly 1.5% of the population. The bicameral Senate gives Wyoming the same representation as California, amplifying the political power of rural producers.

Intrastate distribution is the subject of next lecture (Chapter 4). For today: “the U.S. position” in any negotiation is the position of the domestic coalition with sufficient political voice to define it.

This slide is critical and worth slowing down for. The standard trade-theory presentation talks about country-level gains and losses, which makes it sound like the political problem is just “U.S. vs. China” or “developed vs. developing.” But that’s only half of it. The other half — and arguably the more important half for politics — is within-country distribution. Even when comparative advantage says the U.S. gains overall, those gains go to some American workers, firms, and regions while other American workers, firms, and regions lose. The political question isn’t just “do we gain on net?” — it’s “which Americans gain, and which lose, and which group has more political power?” Big Ag is the clearest U.S. example. Farmers are tiny — about 1.5% of the population. But agriculture is one of the most heavily protected and most heavily subsidized sectors in the U.S. economy. Why? Because the bicameral system in the U.S. Senate gives each state two senators regardless of population, and farm-state senators wield enormous power over farm-state legislation. Wyoming (population 580,000) gets the same Senate representation as California (population 39 million). That structural feature of American political institutions massively amplifies the political power of rural producer interests. We’re going to spend a lot of time on this in Chapter 4 — society-centered theories of trade politics, Stolper-Samuelson, coalitions, the Ricardo-Viner / specific-factors model. For today, just plant the flag: when we say “the U.S. position” in trade negotiations, we don’t mean some abstract national interest. We mean whichever domestic coalition has the political voice to define the U.S. position. That’s a much messier story than the textbook lets on.

The Bargaining Problem

Why Governments Do Not Liberalize Unilaterally

Trade gains exist in principle, but governments rarely cut tariffs without reciprocal concessions. The next slide develops a spatial model of why.

Unilateralism Seems Weak

  • Domestic producer coalitions lose from open markets
  • Unilateral liberalization gives up domestic protection without securing foreign market access for exporters
  • The government moves away from its political ideal point

Bargaining Seems Strong

  • Governments exchange market-access commitments
  • A spatial model (next slide) maps the trade-offs in two dimensions
  • Today we focus on bargaining power and the conditions under which impasses are resolved

Quick recap since we did the bargaining geometry in detail Day 2. The key intuition: if a government just unilaterally drops its tariffs, it gives up the domestic political support of protected producers without getting anything in return for its exporters. So unilateral liberalization moves the government away from its political ideal — which is why governments don’t do it. Liberalization happens through trade agreements where both sides commit. The 1934 Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act in the U.S. is a great example of this logic at work — Congress delegated tariff-cutting authority to the President precisely because reciprocal cuts mobilized exporter constituencies that would otherwise stay silent. We don’t need to do that history again today; just remember the principle. Now we move to two new pieces of analytical equipment: linkage (how impasses get broken), and the signaling vocabulary (cheap talk vs. costly signals).

The Bargaining Space

Each axis is the level of tariff protection one side controls. Bargaining outcomes are points in this space.

Build the diagram with the students. On slide: each circle is an indifference curve around one side’s ideal point, drawn with radius equal to the distance from that ideal point to the status quo — points inside the circle are preferred to the status quo by that side, and where the two circles overlap is the joint-gains lens. That explanation used to live in an on-slide callout; it was cut because the box was overflowing past the bottom of the slide, and the plot’s own labels (status quo, both ideal points, joint-gains lens) already carry the same information visually — narrate the circle/lens logic verbally instead. Two-dimensional issue space: horizontal axis is the level of tariff protection on U.S./EU agriculture, vertical axis is the level of tariff protection on G-20 manufactures. Higher in either direction means more protected. The status quo at Doha was high tariffs in both, so it sits in the upper-right. Each side has an ideal point in the opposite quadrant: the U.S./EU want to keep agricultural protection (high X) while opening up G-20 manufactures (low Y), so the U.S./EU ideal sits in the lower-right. The G-20 wants the mirror: low U.S./EU agricultural protection (low X) and high protection on its own manufactures (high Y), so the G-20 ideal sits in the upper-left. Now draw a circle around each ideal point with radius equal to the distance from that ideal point to the status quo. Any point inside that circle is preferred to the status quo by that side, because it is closer to their ideal point. The intersection of the two circles — the lens — is the set of outcomes both sides prefer to the status quo. That’s where bargaining can produce an agreement. Three minutes here; this is the analytical setup for what comes next. Linkage enlarges the bargaining space by adding issues, expanding the dimensions in which a lens can be found. Costly signals communicate which point in the lens each side will actually accept. Outside options change the location of acceptable deals by changing what “no deal” is worth to each side.

Linkage

Linkage bundles unrelated issues into a single negotiation. It is the principal mechanism by which large multilateral trade rounds reach agreement.

Why single-issue bargaining stalls

  • Each side has a politically protected sector on which it will not concede
  • The two-dimensional bargaining space is too narrow to contain a Pareto improvement
  • Negotiations terminate at the status quo

Why bundled bargaining can succeed

  • Combining agriculture, manufactures, services, and intellectual property creates room for cross-issue trade-offs
  • A concession on agriculture can be offset by a gain on services
  • The Uruguay Round (1986–1994) is the textbook case and produced the WTO itself

The Uruguay Round, the GATS, and the TRIPS agreement exist because linkage allowed issues that could not pass standalone to be bundled with issues on which both sides preferred movement.

Linkage is one of the most important moves in international cooperation theory and it’s worth slowing down on. The intuition: imagine two countries that both refuse to budge on a single issue — say agriculture. They’re stuck. The bargaining space on that one dimension has no Pareto improvement that both sides will accept. But add a second issue, say services or intellectual property, and suddenly the bargaining space expands. One side might be willing to concede on agriculture if they get a big win on services. The Uruguay Round is the textbook case. The U.S. wanted developing countries to commit to intellectual property protection (TRIPS) and services market access (GATS). Developing countries wanted advanced economies to open their agriculture markets and clothing markets. Neither would happen standalone — but bundled together, they did. The WTO itself is a product of that bundling. Without linkage, none of this gets done. Two implications for thinking about contemporary trade politics. First: when negotiations stall on a single issue, you should expect the political path forward to involve bundling — adding issues to expand the space of possible deals. Second: linkage is also why trade agreements have gotten more controversial. Tying together agriculture and IP and labor standards and environmental rules creates richer deals — but also means that anyone with a grievance on any of the linked issues has a reason to oppose the whole package. The trade-policy backlash since the 2000s is partly about linkage.

What Confers Bargaining Power

The largest economy is not always the strongest bargainer. Power comes from the ability to credibly walk away from a deal.

  1. Patience (the “inside option”). Both sides lose from delay, but not equally — political stability and a longer time horizon let a government hold out for more.
  2. Outside options. A good next-best alternative to a deal lets a side hold out and lowers what it will pay for a multilateral concession — the U.S. and EU’s pursuit of TPP and TTIP after 2008 is the example.
  3. Signaling. Cheap talk (“this is our final offer”) is costless and uninformative. Costly signals — walking away, building outside options — actually reveal a side’s position, because a bargainer would not take them otherwise.

Arvind Subramanian (Trade Talks ep. 215, Day 1 podcast) argues RTAs are not only leverage for advanced economies — developing countries can use the same mechanism against China, signaling they will deepen ties elsewhere if China does not open its markets.

Recall Cutler’s Trade Talks episode (Day 2’s podcast, ep. 34, “So You Want to Be a Trade Negotiator”): patience, outside options, and signaling are all about standing firm, but Cutler’s own account stresses the opposite skill too — listening to the other side, letting them air grievances, and building the kind of trust that makes cooperation possible. Bargaining power gets you to the table; getting the other side to actually want to cooperate is a separate skill.

This slide covers three core levers of bargaining power in one pass — talk through the examples rather than reading the list. Start with signaling: each side has private information about its reservation price, and revealing it weakens your position, so everyone has an incentive to misrepresent. That’s why simple verbal claims are cheap talk — costless, and therefore uninformative. Costly signals are what actually work: walking away, building outside options, accepting domestic political pain — actions a bargainer would not take unless the position behind them were real. The 2008 G-20 walkout at Doha (Oatley’s “A Closer Look” box) is the sharpest illustration; students have read it, so just name it, don’t re-explain.

Then patience: both sides lose from delay, but not equally. A government with a stable mandate and a long time horizon can hold out for more; a government facing an election in six months settles for worse. Patience is partly structural, partly strategic — you can sometimes manufacture it by removing yourself from the table.

Then outside options, the parallel mechanism: a good next-best alternative lets you hold out; a bad one means you settle. Once the U.S. and EU started seriously pursuing TPP and TTIP after 2008, the value of a Doha deal to them dropped — a major reason Doha never finished. This is also why the 2008 G-20 walkout didn’t work as a costly signal: the U.S./EU had outside options of their own, so India and China’s threat wasn’t credible.

Close with Subramanian’s twist, from the Day 1 podcast (Trade Talks ep. 215): the textbook tells this story as one-directional — advanced economies have outside options, developing countries don’t — but that’s increasingly not true. Smaller and developing economies can signal willingness to deepen ties with non-Chinese partners, the same mechanism pointed in a different direction. Paraphrase rather than quote; don’t spend more than a sentence or two here, since students already heard the episode.

New Cutler callback, added 2026-07-08: everything on this slide so far is about leverage — holding out, walking away, controlling information. That’s only half of what negotiators actually do, and it’s worth a beat before moving on. Cutler’s Day 2 podcast episode (ep. 34) already made this point once — her account of 25 years negotiating with Japan and Korea emphasizes giving counterparts room to air grievances before problem-solving starts, and building personal credibility and trust over repeated dealings. Bring it back here explicitly: bargaining power gets a government to the table and shapes what it can hold out for, but actually landing a deal often depends on the softer skill of making the other side want to cooperate, not just fear the alternative. The Enforcement Problem

The Prisoner’s Dilemma Applied to Trade

Once an agreement is reached, each side has a unilateral incentive to defect: keep its own tariffs while the other side complies. The prospect of receiving the sucker’s payoff — comply while the other side defects — deters governments from signing in the first place.

The structure

  • Both sides have agreed to comply
  • Either can defect after signing
  • The international system lacks a third-party enforcer
  • What sustains compliance?

Both sides know they would be better off at (Comply, Comply). Individual rationality nonetheless drives them to (Defect, Defect). The equilibrium is collectively suboptimal.

[Slides merged 2026-07-08: the old “Reaching an Agreement Is Not Enough” slide’s right column just narrated in words what this matrix shows, so it’s cut — talk the logic through out loud instead of reading it off the slide, that’s the point of merging these two slides. Comply/Defect replaces Liberalize/Protect throughout, carried forward from here through the “Two Properties” and “Iteration” slides.]

This is the conceptual bridge from bargaining to the WTO solution, and the analytical core of the enforcement section — take real time here. Even if governments find a deal both sides prefer to the status quo, they still face the enforcement problem: once they sign, each side has a unilateral incentive to defect — keep its own protected sectors closed while exploiting the other side’s market opening. The fear of the sucker’s payoff (you complied, they didn’t) is enough to prevent cooperation from starting in the first place.

Walk the matrix first, then the reasoning. G-20 is the row player, EU is the column player, each with two strategies, Comply or Defect. Four cells: (C, C) mutual compliance with joint gains; (C, D) G-20 complies but EU does not — G-20’s worst outcome, the sucker’s payoff; (D, C) G-20 defects while EU complies — G-20’s best outcome, the temptation payoff; (D, D) mutual defection, the status quo. Preference order for G-20: (D, C) > (C, C) > (D, D) > (C, D). EU is symmetric.

Best-response logic, talked through rather than read: if the EU complies, G-20 compares (C, C) and (D, C) and prefers to defect, because (D, C) is its best outcome. If the EU defects, G-20 compares (C, D) and (D, D) and prefers to defect, because (C, D) is its worst outcome. Defect is therefore a strict dominant strategy for G-20 — optimal regardless of what the EU does. By symmetry, the same logic makes defect dominant for the EU too. Both rational players defect, and the equilibrium is (D, D).

Now name the equilibrium. (D, D) is a Nash equilibrium because neither player can unilaterally improve their payoff by deviating — unilateral deviation from (D, D) yields the sucker’s payoff for the deviator. The result is a stable but suboptimal outcome: both players prefer (C, C), but neither can move there alone. This is the formal expression of the cooperation problem and the setup for everything in Section 5: how iteration and reciprocity let governments escape this trap.

Two Properties of the (D, D) Equilibrium

Pareto suboptimal

At least one player could be made better off without making anyone else worse off.

  • Both sides prefer (C, C) to (D, D)
  • (C, C) is reachable in principle
  • Yet they don’t reach it

Nash equilibrium

No player can unilaterally improve their payoff by changing strategy.

  • G-20 deviation from (D, D) yields (C, D), its worst outcome
  • EU deviation from (D, D) yields (D, C), its worst outcome
  • Neither player has a unilateral incentive to deviate

Together, these properties define the cooperation problem. The (Defect, Defect) outcome is stable (no unilateral incentive to deviate) yet collectively suboptimal (both sides prefer Comply, Comply). Individual rationality produces collective dysfunction.

Two formal definitions worth nailing down. Pareto suboptimal: an outcome from which at least one player could be made better off without making anyone else worse off. (D, D) is Pareto suboptimal because both players strictly prefer (C, C). There’s a Pareto improvement available, but the game doesn’t get there. Nash equilibrium: an outcome at which no player can unilaterally improve their payoff by changing strategy. (D, D) is a Nash equilibrium because if either player switched alone to comply, they’d get the sucker’s payoff. The deviation makes them worse off. So neither has any incentive to deviate. The kicker: these two properties together are what makes the cooperation problem hard. (D, D) is stable (Nash) AND bad (Pareto suboptimal). It’s stable because it’s bad — if it were Pareto optimal, no one would want to deviate; here, deviating unilaterally makes you worse off even though deviating jointly would make everyone better. This is the formal definition of what it means to be trapped. Conceptual point for students: when you see “X happens even though Y would be better for everyone,” the underlying analytical structure is almost always something like this — individually-rational behavior producing collectively-irrational outcomes. The PD is a workhorse model not just for trade, but for climate, security, public goods provision, antitrust, you name it.

The WTO as Incentive-Compatible Enforcement

Iteration

A one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma traps countries in a suboptimal equilibrium (Defect, Defect). But states are long-lasting: they play repeatedly, and often with the same partners. In an iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, cooperation can become rational under certain conditions.

One-shot game

  • Defection yields the temptation payoff once
  • No future round, no future punishment
  • Cooperation is irrational

Iterated game

  • Defection today invites punishment tomorrow
  • The stream of cooperation payoffs (C, C) can exceed a one-time defection gain
  • Cooperation is sustainable if players are sufficiently patient
  • This result is the Folk Theorem in game theory

Iteration changes everything. In a one-shot PD, defection is rational because there’s no future. You take the temptation payoff and move on. But in real life, the U.S. and EU don’t negotiate once — they negotiate over and over, year after year, decade after decade. Iteration introduces a future. If you defect today, your partner can punish tomorrow. That changes your payoff calculation. You’re no longer comparing one defection against one cooperation — you’re comparing a stream of future cooperation payoffs against a single defection payoff plus future punishment. If you discount the future at a reasonable rate and your partner uses a credible punishment strategy, cooperation becomes the rational choice. This is the analytical foundation of every cooperative institution we have. The Folk Theorem in game theory makes this formal: in an iterated PD with sufficiently patient players and credible punishment strategies, cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium. Note: the result depends on patience (low enough discount rate) and a credible punishment strategy. We need to talk about both — and we’ll see in a moment that the WTO institutionally supplies (or at least supports) both conditions.

Three Conditions for Cooperation (Axelrod-Keohane-Oye)

1. Iteration

The same parties must play repeatedly and expect future interactions. Without iteration, no future round can discipline present behavior.

2. Reciprocity

Players need a strategy that rewards cooperation and punishes defection. The classic example is Axelrod’s tit-for-tat.

3. Future-orientation

Players must value future payoffs enough that anticipated punishment disciplines current behavior. A sufficiently low discount rate sustains cooperation; a high one collapses the game back to one-shot.

Robert Axelrod (1984), The Evolution of Cooperation; Robert Keohane (1984), After Hegemony; Kenneth Oye (1986), Cooperation Under Anarchy.

Three conditions, each citing the foundational works in international cooperation theory. Iteration: the same parties have to play repeatedly. If you only deal with someone once, they have no reason to keep their word — and you have no way to punish them later. The future has to exist in some real sense for the game theory to work. Reciprocity: the cooperator needs a strategy that rewards cooperation and punishes defection in measurable ways. Tit-for-tat — play whatever your partner played last round — is the best-known example, and Axelrod’s computer tournaments in the early 1980s showed it consistently outperforms more complicated strategies. The key feature: nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear. Future-orientation: the players have to care about future payoffs. If they fully discount the future — if a payoff tomorrow is worth zero today — the iterated game collapses back to a one-shot game and cooperation fails. Bring the citations: Axelrod’s Evolution of Cooperation (1984) is the empirical and theoretical foundation. Keohane’s After Hegemony (1984) is the IR application. Oye’s edited volume Cooperation Under Anarchy (1986) brought together a generation of scholars working in this tradition. These are the texts that defined how IR thinks about cooperation for the next 40 years. The WTO is, in many ways, the institutional embodiment of this literature.

How the WTO Supplies These Conditions

Iteration

  • Stable membership: 164 members and few exits in over seventy years
  • Repeated rounds: eight completed, the ninth (Doha) ongoing
  • Periodic policy reviews of every member by its peers

The same governments deal with each other repeatedly, year after year.

Reciprocity (information and standards)

  • MFN and national treatment supply bright-line standards against which behavior can be evaluated
  • Transparency requirements apply to trade remedies, safeguards, and non-tariff barriers
  • The WTO publishes systematic data on every member’s tariffs and NTBs

Tit-for-tat requires that each side observe when its partner has defected. The WTO supplies the information.

Future-orientation is a property of governments themselves, not something the WTO can supply. When governments stop valuing future trade cooperation, the iterative logic of the institution erodes. (More on this when the student presents Bown & Keynes 2020.)

This is the central conceptual argument of the lecture’s second half. The WTO doesn’t enforce trade agreements directly — it supplies the institutional conditions under which governments can enforce agreements among themselves. How? By providing iteration and information. Iteration: the WTO has had stable membership for decades. 164 countries belong; almost no one has ever left. So when the U.S. and Brazil negotiate today, they know they’ll be negotiating again next year and the year after. There’s a stream of future interactions to discipline current behavior. The WTO also runs regular bargaining rounds (eight completed, plus Doha ongoing) and conducts trade policy reviews for every member every few years. Every member’s behavior is under continual scrutiny. Reciprocity needs information. To play tit-for-tat, you have to know when your partner has cheated. The WTO provides this through several institutional features. MFN and National Treatment are bright-line rules — you can tell when someone violates them. Trade remedies, safeguards, and NTBs all have transparency requirements — countries have to notify the WTO when they impose them. And the WTO secretariat publishes systematic data on tariffs and trade barriers. This is the information infrastructure that makes credible reciprocity possible. What the WTO can NOT supply is the third condition: future-orientation. That’s a property of governments themselves. When governments stop valuing future cooperation — when they discount the future heavily, when they prioritize short-term political wins — the entire iterated game can collapse. This is foreshadowing for the student presentation: the Appellate Body crisis is, in part, a story about the U.S. government deciding it values the future of WTO cooperation less than it values the freedom to act unilaterally now.

The DSM as Institutionalized Tit-for-Tat

The Dispute Settlement Mechanism (DSM) is the WTO’s formalized tit-for-tat. It legalizes retaliation, requires that retaliation be proportionate to the harm, and embeds the entire process in a predictable timeline. These features make WTO enforcement incentive-compatible: every member knows in advance the cost of defection.

Why incentive-compatible

  • The cost of defection (authorized retaliation) is known in advance
  • The punishment is proportional to the harm caused
  • The process is transparent and rule-bound
  • Governments can rationally weigh compliance against the predictable price of non-compliance

The big conceptual claim of this section. The DSM is the WTO’s most distinctive institutional innovation, and it’s best understood as institutionalized tit-for-tat. The DSM doesn’t punish violators directly — instead, it legalizes the injured party’s retaliation, but constrains it to be proportionate and rule-bound. The phrase to drill into students’ heads: “incentive-compatible enforcement.” The point is that under DSM rules, every member knows ex ante what defection costs. There’s a predictable process, shown at right: consultations (60 days), a panel ruling (up to 9 months), an optional appellate review (up to 90 days — the stage that’s now broken, shown in crimson), and then implementation or authorized retaliation if there’s no compliance. The whole process runs about 15 months from complaint to retaliation. A government considering whether to violate WTO commitments can rationally compare: comply, or pay the predictable price. That’s what makes it incentive-compatible — the institutional setup is designed so that rational governments will (mostly) choose compliance.

Retaliation does not escalate into a trade war because it’s rule-bound: retaliation must be authorized by the DSB, its magnitude is bounded by demonstrated harm, and the procedural constraints prevent the spiral that produced Smoot-Hawley in 1930. Brazil, in the cotton case coming up, could retaliate against the U.S. only up to the amount of harm U.S. policy caused — the institution constrains retaliation to be measured. That’s how international rules turn a destructive logic into a productive one.

The WTO Is Not a State

“The WTO is not an international equivalent of a state because the WTO does not have the authority or the capacity to punish governments that fail to comply.” (Oatley, p. 70)

What the WTO cannot do

  • Levy fines or sanctions on a member
  • Compel a member to change policy
  • Override domestic law
  • Maintain anything resembling a police force

What the WTO does instead

  • Provides rules members have agreed to
  • Provides information about compliance
  • Provides a dispute mechanism that allows members to enforce agreements against one another
  • Activates domestic political coalitions inside a non-compliant country (next slide)

The WTO provides infrastructure for self-enforcement among its members. This distinction matters for understanding both how the cotton case worked and why the Appellate Body crisis matters.

This is one of the most important conceptual moves in the entire chapter, and Oatley puts it in the conclusion. The WTO is not a state. It doesn’t have sovereign authority. It can’t fine, sanction, or compel anyone. So what does it do? It provides infrastructure. Rules that members agree to follow. Information about who’s complying and who isn’t. A formal dispute mechanism that helps members enforce against each other. And — this is the part Oatley underplays but we’ll emphasize in the cotton case — it activates domestic political coalitions inside violating countries to push their own government to comply. So when critics say “the WTO has no teeth” — meaning it can’t directly punish — they’re correct in a narrow sense but wrong in a more important sense. The teeth are in the violator’s domestic politics. WTO authorization of retaliation creates losers from non-compliance inside the violating country, who then lobby their own government for resolution. The next section makes that mechanism concrete with the Brazilian cotton case. Keep this distinction in mind for the article presentation as well — Bown & Keynes talk about how the U.S. dismantled the Appellate Body, and one of their key claims is that the U.S. did so precisely because the AB had teeth (politically inconvenient ones for the U.S.).

The DSM in Action: U.S.–Brazil Cotton

The Cotton Case — and Its Limits Today

Why this case is weaker evidence today

  • The 2018 Farm Bill returned seed cotton to the same type of price-support programs that prompted Brazil’s original complaint, reversing the insurance-only fix
  • The Appellate Body — the mechanism that made this whole story work — has lacked a quorum since December 2019

Broken for the U.S., Not for Everyone

  • In 2020, 61 WTO members — not the U.S. — formed the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA), using Article 25 arbitration as a substitute appeal process
  • The MPIA has issued two binding awards since 2020 (Colombia–EU frozen fries, 2022; EU–China IP enforcement, 2025)
  • Since December 2019, roughly 32 panel rulings have been appealed “into the void” and left unenforced — but most panel rulings are still adopted without ever being appealed

[Slide compressed 2026-07-08: this one slide replaces what was six slides — Case at a Glance, Cotton Case Timeline, How the DSM Created Pressure for Compliance, Brazil’s Strategic Targeting, Reassessing the “No Teeth” Critique, and Where the DSM Stands Today. Updated 2026-07-08: the top callout became this compact timeline chart, and the “Up next” column was replaced with MPIA/post-2019 dispute data. Full narrative for delivery, condensed from all six:

Brazil filed its WTO complaint in 2002, alleging U.S. cotton subsidies — export credit support plus domestic price and income supports — depressed global cotton prices and violated WTO rules. The U.S. defended the subsidies as a domestic safety net, not an export subsidy in disguise. The case ran 12 years: a 2004 panel ruling against the U.S., a 2005 Appellate Body ruling upholding it, a 2007 compliance panel finding U.S. adjustments still insufficient, and in 2009 WTO authorization for Brazil to retaliate up to $823 million per year — a developing country formally cleared to impose punitive tariffs on the world’s largest economy. (Narrate this while the timeline is on screen — the chart shows the four anchor dates; the panel/appeal detail and the 2014 settlement figures are verbal.)

The mechanism that matters most: Brazil did not target retaliation at random. It picked U.S. export industries that were politically organized — strong trade associations, active lobbyists, deep congressional ties — over diffuse, unorganized ones that would have generated economic harm but no political pressure. When the targeted industries faced credible tariff threats, they mobilized fast and pressed Congress directly. The 2014 Farm Bill became the legislative vehicle for compliance: cotton support was restructured into an insurance program (STAX), and a final settlement (October 2014, $300 million lump sum to the Brazilian Cotton Institute) closed the case.

That’s the payoff for the “no teeth” critique of the WTO. Yes, the WTO cannot directly punish a member — no fines, no asset seizure, no compelled policy change. But that’s the wrong test. The right test is whether it changes behavior, and here it did: authorized retaliation, aimed strategically, created domestic losers from non-compliance who did the enforcement work through their own political system. A developing country brought the most powerful WTO member into compliance without any direct power over U.S. policy.

Now the complication, new to this version of the lecture: this case is weaker evidence today than it was a few years ago, for two separate reasons. First, the 2018 Farm Bill (via seed-cotton provisions dating to the Bipartisan Budget Act of February 2018, carried into the December 2018 Farm Bill) added cotton back into the ARC/PLC price-support programs — the same category of program at the center of Brazil’s original complaint. CRS analysis doesn’t call this an outright violation of the 2014 settlement, but it reverses the insurance-only fix and reopens WTO exposure. Second, and more fundamentally: the Appellate Body — the mechanism that made the entire cotton case possible — has been non-functional since December 2019, because the U.S. has refused to approve new appointments.

But — and this is the nuance worth landing before the handoff — “broken” is a claim about the U.S. specifically, not about the whole system. In 2020, a group of WTO members (61 as of 2026, including the EU, China, Canada, Japan, Brazil, and most of Latin America — but not the U.S.) built a workaround: the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement, which uses DSU Article 25 arbitration as a substitute for the missing Appellate Body. It has issued two binding awards so far (Colombia-EU frozen fries, 2022; EU-China IP enforcement, 2025) — slow, but functioning, for its members. Meanwhile, since December 2019, roughly 32 panel rulings have been “appealed into the void” and left without legal effect — but that also means most panel rulings are not appealed at all and get adopted normally. So the system hasn’t stopped; it has fragmented. It works for members willing to use the MPIA, and it doesn’t work — for anyone — against a party that appeals into the void, which is the option the U.S. has kept for itself by staying outside the MPIA.

That’s the transition: if the WTO’s teeth were always in the violator’s domestic politics, and the U.S. has both disabled the appeal mechanism and declined the workaround everyone else built, what happens to the incentive-compatibility story we just built? That’s exactly the question Bown & Keynes (2020) take up next.

Sources for the MPIA/void figures (verify before class, not yet independently cross-checked beyond initial search): WTO’s own MPIA page (61 participants, March 2026 count); reporting on the Colombia frozen-fries award (Dec. 2022) and the EU-China IP enforcement award (July 2025); the ~32-ruling “appealed into the void” figure as of April 2025; and a Jan. 2020-April 2024 tally of 38 panel reports circulated, 21 appealed, 8 adopted without appeal.]

Bown & Keynes (2020): Article Overview

Element Bown & Keynes (2020)
Research Question Why did the US decide to dismantle the DSM's Appellate Body?
Main Claim The end point of two decades of bipartisan U.S. frustration that the DSM kept striking down America's preferred trade-remedy tools (antidumping, countervailing duties, safeguards) while requiring the U.S. to carry the hardest, highest-burden 'systemic' cases -- especially against China -- largely alone.
Argument Space Complicates two standard stories: that the U.S. exit was pure nationalist hostility to international law (frustration with the Appellate Body dates to 1995 and spans both parties), and that the DSM is a neutral, incentive-compatible enforcer that sustains cooperation regardless of power (it became politically unsustainable for the very state whose continued buy-in it needed most).
Evidence Used Original data construction from WTO and U.S. trade records: import-coverage shares for VERs, antidumping, countervailing duties, and safeguards (1972-2019); dispute counts by type, complainant, and defendant (1995-2017). Supplemented with USTR testimony, congressional records, and the authors' own interviews (their Trade Talks podcast).
Implications The Appellate Body's paralysis reflects a structural asymmetry in how the DSM's costs and benefits were distributed, not just one administration's preferences. A durable fix likely requires rebalancing access to trade remedies, not simply reappointing judges -- and the system has already fragmented into an MPIA track and a non-MPIA track. For international organizations more broadly, the lesson is that keeping powerful members inside the system may require watching for institutional over-reach against those members, not just enforcing rules evenhandedly.

This slide follows the same overview format used for the Rickard article on Day 2 (a five-row table: research question, main claim, argument space, evidence, implications) — same structure, applied to a very different kind of article. Where Rickard was a quantitative text-as-data study, Bown & Keynes is closer to an analytic-narrative/political-history piece: archival material (USTR testimony, congressional hearings dating to 1995), original interviews (their own podcast), and original data construction from WTO and U.S. trade records. Walk the table top to bottom. Research question: the WTO’s dispute settlement mechanism (DSM) was supposed to be the model of incentive-compatible enforcement we just spent Section 5 building — so why did the U.S., its architect, choose to dismantle its own Appellate Body? Main claim: frustration with the Appellate Body’s rulings on U.S. trade remedies is bipartisan and dates back to 1995 (Lighthizer and Alan Wolff were pushing withdrawal legislation under a Republican senator that year; the complaints repeat under both parties for two decades). Argument space: this directly tests the model built earlier today. If the DSM is genuinely incentive-compatible, it should be stable regardless of which member is constrained by it — but Bown & Keynes show that even the most powerful member, the one that designed the rules, found the mechanism politically unsustainable once it started reliably constraining that member’s own preferred tools. That’s a real complication for the “the WTO supplies the conditions for cooperation” story, not just a footnote. Evidence gets one line here — the next slide unpacks the two clearest empirical results with figures from the article itself. Implications: land the added sentence explicitly — this is the broader takeaway for institutional design generally, not just the WTO. An international organization that wants to keep its most powerful members inside the tent may need to watch for its own over-reach against them, the same way it watches for member noncompliance — keeping a hegemon in a system it could otherwise exit takes more than enforcing rules against it evenhandedly.

Delivery notes: left figure — nearly two-thirds (65%) of the 141 disputes brought against the U.S. between 1995 and January 2017 concerned trade remedies, versus barely a third (33%) of disputes among other members, and the U.S. typically lost these. This is the “no teeth” reassessment from the cotton case running in reverse: the same enforcement mechanism that made Brazil’s cotton complaint work also relentlessly constrained America’s own favorite trade-defense tools, over and over, for two decades. Right figure — when the U.S. brought cases against China over systemic issues (state subsidies, IP enforcement, not just one-off antidumping actions), it usually did so alone; in 11 of its 18 cases, no other WTO member joined. Bown & Keynes quote former USTR Michael Froman: other members were “very happy to have us go and pursue these issues with China” but “don’t necessarily invest a huge amount of effort themselves.” Put the two figures together and the argument lands: the U.S. was disproportionately the target of the DSM’s enforcement on trade remedies, and disproportionately the one supplying enforcement effort against China — carrying more of the burden while facing more of the cost. Bown & Keynes argue that combination is what made the system politically unsustainable for Washington specifically, more than any single administration’s ideology.

Discussion questions, same slide: Question 1 — the U.S.’s complaints. Confirmed complaints from the article, in rough order of concreteness: (a) the U.S. lost repeatedly and specifically on its favorite trade-remedy tools — safeguards, antidumping (especially “zeroing,” which the U.S. lost to the EU in 2003 and Japan in 2004 across dozens of cases), and countervailing duties; (b) a belief, voiced by American critics going back to 2000 (Lighthizer and Alan Wolff’s joint Senate testimony) and restated by trade negotiator John Greenwald in 2003, that panelists were “making policy” rather than neutrally interpreting the text members had actually negotiated — i.e., a judicial-overreach complaint, not just a losing-record complaint; (c) a sense of double standard: 65% of disputes brought against the U.S. concerned trade remedies versus only 33% among other members (Figure 3), so the U.S. felt uniquely exposed on the tools it valued most. If a question comes up about the specific 2018 USTR list (China non-market-economy treatment in antidumping, Chinese SOEs as public bodies in countervailing duty cases, safeguards under GATT Article XIX, the Byrd Amendment, plus non-remedy issues like Country of Origin Labelling and the Foreign Sales Corporation tax dispute) — that’s in the paper’s footnote 4, worth mentioning if students ask for specifics but not essential to memorize.

Question 2 — Lighthizer and the structure/agency tension. This is the deliberate callback to Day 2’s Rickard/Cutler discussion, and worth naming explicitly. The structural story explains why frustration built for two decades (bipartisan, dating to 1995) — that’s the “why now was possible” account. But it takes a specific person to actually pull the trigger, and Lighthizer is that person: a longtime steel-industry trade lawyer who was already pushing WTO-withdrawal legislation as a Senate aide in 1995, appointed USTR in 2017 with exactly the ideological priors and industry ties to act on that frustration. That’s the same point Cutler’s podcast made about negotiators generally — structures set the range of possible outcomes, but which outcome actually happens depends on the specific individual holding the pen, their granular knowledge, and their prior commitments. Push students to consider the counterfactual: would a different USTR appointment under the same Trump administration, or the same USTR under a different administration, have produced the same outcome? The article doesn’t fully answer this (it’s a positive account, not a counterfactual one), so this is a genuinely open question to debate, not one with a clean answer in the text.

Question 3 — where trade-remedy preferences come from. This is the most theoretical of the three and ties back to the course’s levels-of-analysis material (Chapter 4’s within-country distributional politics, covered earlier today). Give students three live options rather than a single answer: (a) societal/interest-group capture — import-competing industries (steel above all; note Lighthizer and Wolff were both career steel-industry trade lawyers) lobby to create and defend trade-remedy statutes that benefit them specifically; (b) bureaucratic/state-centric autonomy — USTR and Commerce administer these laws and may have developed an institutional stake in preserving them independent of which industries are lobbying in a given year; (c) something else — path dependence (U.S. antidumping law dates to 1916/1921; once a legal apparatus exists, defending it becomes a matter of institutional and legal identity, not fresh cost-benefit calculation each time) or an ideational/nationalist framing (trade remedies as a matter of sovereign prerogative, echoing the “aggressively unilateral” Section 301 tradition the article opens with). The article’s own evidence leans toward (a) and (c) together — Lighthizer/Wolff’s steel-industry background plus the article’s opening framing of Section 301 as a decades-old pattern — but doesn’t rule out (b). Good moment to let students disagree without resolving it for them.

Writing Prompt: Can the WTO Survive Without an Appellate Body?

Writing prompt. The cotton case worked because the DSM turned a diffuse trade grievance into a credible, incentive-compatible threat — Brazil could target U.S. cotton subsidies precisely because the ruling was enforceable. Bown and Keynes just showed you that the Appellate Body has had no working quorum since December 2019.

Using the three conditions for cooperation from today — iteration, reciprocity, and future-orientation — make an argument: has the WTO’s capacity to sustain cooperation actually collapsed, or does the underlying game still hold even without a functioning appeals body? Name the strongest piece of evidence against your own position.

Write for about fifteen minutes. Use at least two of the three conditions by name. Then we’ll discuss as a class.

Run this right after the presenter finishes with Bown and Keynes and the class discussion winds down. The point is to force students to actually use the iterated-PD apparatus rather than just report that “the WTO is in crisis” — that’s the easy, non-analytical answer. The strong students will notice that iteration and the shadow of the future don’t strictly require a functioning Appellate Body — states can still retaliate unilaterally, reputational costs still accrue — but they will also see that removing the impartial adjudicator changes who gets to decide whether a violation occurred, which reintroduces exactly the enforcement problem section four described. There’s no clean answer; grade on whether they use the vocabulary correctly and engage the counterargument, not on which side they land on.
