From Vulnerability to Weapon

Section 1

Yesterday’s Web, Turned Into a Weapon

The debt and finance unit asked how interdependence makes a state vulnerable — open up, and you can be hit by a crisis you didn’t cause.

Today we flip the question:

  • The same connections are levers another state can pull
  • Economic statecraft — using economic means to pursue strategic/political goals
  • Economic coercion — deliberately imposing or threatening economic costs to change a target’s behavior

The pivot. Interdependence is not just a condition you are exposed to. It is a resource that the well-positioned state can convert into power over others.

Same plumbing, two readings: a pipe that can carry a shock toward you, or a valve someone else can close on you.

Start by naming the pivot out loud, because the intellectual move today is a deliberate reversal of everything we just did. In the debt and capital-flows unit, interdependence was a hazard you were exposed to. You opened your capital account, the money flowed in, and then — through no fault of your own, sometimes triggered entirely abroad, by a Volcker shock or a shift in global sentiment — the money rushed out and you had a crisis. Interdependence there was a condition that made you fragile. The state was on the receiving end of forces it did not control.

Today the same web becomes an instrument that a state can pick up and use against another state on purpose. Two terms anchor this. Economic statecraft is the broad umbrella — the use of economic means, carrots and sticks, to pursue strategic or political objectives. Economic coercion is the sharper, sticks end of that: deliberately imposing economic costs, or credibly threatening to, in order to change what a target government does. Sanctions are the most familiar form, but coercion is broader than sanctions, as we will see.

The image I want you to carry is in the bottom callout. Yesterday interdependence was a pipe — a channel along which a shock could travel toward you. Today it is a valve — a channel that whoever controls the junction can close on you. It is literally the same plumbing; what changed is whose hand is on it and what they intend. That reframe is the whole day. Everything that follows is about which states get to put their hand on the valve, how, and with what consequences.

Coercion Is Old; Today’s Version Is New

Using economic leverage for political ends is ancient — blockades, embargoes, denial of trade.

What is distinctive now:

  • The global economy is dense and networked, not a set of bilateral trade ties
  • A few hubs carry an outsized share of the world’s money, data, and goods
  • Whoever controls a hub gains leverage over everyone who depends on it

The two readings of the day. Farrell & Newman give us the structure — how networks create coercive power. Drezner gives us the evidence — whether using that power actually works.

Coercion isn’t only the dramatic embargo. It runs quietly through the everyday infrastructure of finance, information, and supply chains.

Before we get into the theory, let me situate it, because students sometimes hear “economic coercion” and assume it is a brand-new phenomenon invented in the last decade. It is not. States have used economic leverage for political ends for as long as there have been states — naval blockades, embargoes, cutting off a rival’s grain or oil. The instinct to starve an adversary economically is ancient.

What is genuinely new is the structure of the modern global economy, and this is exactly what Farrell and Newman are theorizing. We do not live in a world of separate bilateral trade relationships any longer. We live in a world of dense, interconnected networks — for finance, for information, for supply chains — and crucially those networks are not flat. They are organized around a small number of hubs that carry an enormously outsized share of the traffic. Most cross-border payments touch the dollar system. A huge share of international financial messaging runs through one Belgian cooperative called SWIFT. Advanced semiconductors come from a tiny handful of firms. When the network looks like that, controlling a hub gives you leverage not over one trading partner but over everyone who depends on the hub. That is the new thing.

So the two readings divide the labor of the day cleanly, and the top callout names it. Farrell and Newman supply the structure — a theory of how the topology of global networks creates coercive power and who gets to wield it. Drezner supplies the evidence — a careful look at whether, when states actually reach for these tools, the tools work. Structure first, then effectiveness. And keep the bottom callout in mind: most of this coercion is not the cinematic total embargo. It runs quietly through the boring infrastructure — the payment rails, the messaging systems, the chip supply chains — that we normally never think about precisely because they work.

Weaponized Interdependence

Section 2 · Farrell & Newman 2019

Networks Have Hubs — and Hubs Have Power

Farrell & Newman’s core insight: global economic networks are not flat.

  • They have an asymmetric topology — a few central hubs, many peripheral nodes
  • Hubs arise from efficiency: it is cheaper to route everyone’s payments, data, and trade through shared infrastructure
  • A state with jurisdiction over a hub can convert that structural centrality into coercive power

The key claim. Power doesn’t come from how much you trade. It comes from where you sit in the network — control of the node everyone else routes through.

This is a structural theory of power: the map of the network, not the wealth of any one country, is what does the explaining.

Here is the single most important idea in the Farrell and Newman article, and I want you to be able to state it crisply. Their claim is that global economic networks are not flat — they have an asymmetric topology. Picture a map of who-connects-to-whom for, say, international payments. It does not look like a uniform mesh where everyone has a direct link to everyone else. It looks like an airline route map: a small number of giant hubs that almost everything routes through, and a large number of peripheral nodes that connect to the rest of the world via those hubs.

Why do networks take that shape? Efficiency, mostly. It is far cheaper to build shared infrastructure that everyone routes through than to maintain a separate bespoke link between every pair of countries. So banks settle through a few correspondent institutions; financial messages flow through one dominant messaging cooperative; the dollar becomes the common currency that everyone’s trades clear in. Nobody designed these hubs as weapons. They emerged because they were efficient. But — and this is the move — once a hub exists, the state that has legal jurisdiction over it inherits enormous leverage. If the dominant payment messaging system is headquartered in your jurisdiction, or the dominant clearing currency is yours, you can reach into that hub and pull levers that touch everyone connected to it.

Notice what kind of theory this is, in the bottom callout: it is structural. The thing that explains a state’s coercive power here is not how rich it is or how much it trades in raw volume. It is where it sits in the network — whether it controls the node that everyone else has to route through. A medium-sized economy that happens to host a critical hub can have far more coercive reach than a larger economy sitting out on the periphery. Position beats size. Hold that, because it is what makes this more than just “big countries push small countries around.”

Two Things You Can Do From a Hub

Effect What it lets you do Real-world example
Panopticon effect Surveillance — you can SEE the transactions flowing through the hub U.S. access to SWIFT data tracked terrorist financing after 9/11
Chokepoint effect Denial — you can CUT a target off from the hub entirely 2012: Iran's banks were disconnected from SWIFT; 2022: Russian banks de-SWIFTed

SWIFT is the messaging layer of global finance — the cooperative that tells banks who is paying whom. Control over it delivers both effects at once: watch the flows, or sever them.

Farrell and Newman say a state that controls a hub can do two distinct things, and you should keep them separate in your head because they are different kinds of power. The first is the panopticon effect. A panopticon, from Bentham and then Foucault, is a structure designed so that one observer at the center can watch everyone without being seen. A network hub is a panopticon: because the world’s transactions route through it, whoever has jurisdiction over the hub can see those transactions. This is surveillance power — informational. The classic example is SWIFT after 9/11: U.S. intelligence gained access to SWIFT messaging data and used it to trace terrorist financing across borders. Nobody was cut off; the United States was simply watching the flows that passed through the hub.

The second is the chokepoint effect. A chokepoint is a valve. Because peripheral actors depend on the hub to function, whoever controls the hub can deny access — cut a target off entirely. This is denial power, and it is the dramatic one. The two cleanest examples are in the table. In 2012, under U.S. and European pressure, SWIFT disconnected Iranian banks, which made it enormously harder for Iran to move money internationally. And in 2022, after the invasion of Ukraine, a set of major Russian banks were cut off from SWIFT as part of the Western sanctions response. In both cases, the target was severed from the messaging infrastructure that the entire global banking system relies on.

The reason SWIFT is the textbook case — the bottom callout — is that it is the messaging layer of global finance. It does not move money itself; it is the standardized system that tells banks who is paying whom, the instructions that let cross-border payments happen at all. It is a Belgian cooperative, so it sits under European jurisdiction, and it is so central that being cut off from it is like being cut off from the financial dial tone. One hub, both effects: you can watch everything that flows through it, the panopticon, or you can shut a target out of it, the chokepoint. Now let us look at the other great hub, which is even more powerful.

The Dollar System as the Master Hub

The U.S. dollar is the most powerful chokepoint of all:

  • Most cross-border trade and finance is invoiced and settled in dollars — connecting back to Chapter 10’s “exorbitant privilege”
  • Dollar payments ultimately clear through U.S. banks (correspondent banking)
  • That gives U.S. authorities legal reach over almost any dollar transaction, anywhere
  • A foreign bank that helps a sanctioned entity risks losing dollar access — a corporate death sentence

Why the dollar is the ultimate weapon. You don’t have to trade with the U.S. to be exposed. If your transaction touches the dollar — and almost everything does — it touches U.S. jurisdiction.

This is secondary sanctions: the U.S. threatens third parties who deal with the target, leveraging everyone’s need for the dollar.

If SWIFT is the messaging hub, the dollar is the master hub, and it is where weaponized interdependence is most powerful. Connect this straight back to the monetary unit — Chapter 10, the dollar’s central role, the “exorbitant privilege.” The dollar is not just America’s currency; it is the world’s invoicing and settlement currency. A staggering share of international trade is priced in dollars, a huge share of cross-border lending is in dollars, and central banks hold their reserves overwhelmingly in dollars. That dominance was built for economic reasons — the dollar is liquid, stable, and everyone already uses it, so everyone keeps using it.

Here is the coercive payoff. Dollar payments, even between two non-American parties, ultimately clear through the U.S. banking system, through correspondent accounts that foreign banks hold with American banks. That technical fact — clearing runs through New York — means that almost any dollar transaction on earth passes through U.S. jurisdiction at some point. And that gives U.S. authorities legal reach over transactions that have nothing to do with the United States as a trading partner. A bank in a third country that processes payments for a sanctioned entity can be told: do that, and you lose your access to the dollar clearing system. For a major bank, losing dollar access is essentially a death sentence — you cannot be a serious international bank without it.

That mechanism is what the bottom callout calls secondary sanctions, and it is the thing that makes the dollar the ultimate weapon. Primary sanctions say “Americans may not deal with the target.” Secondary sanctions say “anyone, anywhere, who deals with the target will themselves be cut off from the dollar.” Because everyone needs the dollar, the United States can effectively conscript the entire world’s banks into enforcing its sanctions, even against the wishes of their own governments. You do not have to trade with America to be exposed; you only have to touch the dollar, and almost everything does. That is the panopticon and the chokepoint operating at the scale of the whole financial system. And — foreshadowing section five — it is also exactly the power that gets eroded if it is overused.

Do Sanctions Work?

Section 3 · Drezner 2024

The Sanctions Debate Has Moved

Drezner reviews where the field has gone since Baldwin’s Economic Statecraft (1985):

  • Old consensus: “sanctions rarely work” — costly signals that mostly fail to change behavior
  • Newer findings: economic coercion is more effective than once believed, once we measure carefully
  • Coercion is likelier to succeed when it is multilateral, institutionalized, and hits constituencies the target government cares about

Drezner’s headline. Sanctions work better than the old pessimism claimed — but their unintended costs and externalities are far larger than once understood.

Two corrections at once: less pessimistic about effectiveness, more worried about side effects. Don’t collapse them into one.

Now we move from structure to evidence — from Farrell and Newman’s “here is how the power works” to Drezner’s “here is what we actually know about whether using it accomplishes anything.” Drezner’s article is a review essay, so it is taking stock of an entire literature, and the natural starting point is David Baldwin’s 1985 book “Economic Statecraft,” which set the agenda for the modern study of sanctions. For a long time after Baldwin, the dominant impression — sometimes a caricature of the careful work — was pessimistic: sanctions rarely work. The line you would hear is that sanctions are mostly expressive, a way for leaders to look tough domestically while the target government simply absorbs the pain and refuses to change.

Drezner’s review says that picture has shifted, and you should be precise about how. As scholars got better at measuring outcomes — accounting for cases where the mere threat of sanctions produced concessions before sanctions were ever imposed, and being careful about what success even means — the evidence turned out to be less pessimistic. Economic coercion is more effective than the old consensus held. And the literature has learned when it works better: coercion is likelier to succeed when it is multilateral rather than unilateral, especially when the cooperation is institutionalized and includes the target’s major trading partners, and when the sanctions bite the specific domestic constituencies that the target government actually depends on. So it is not “sanctions never work” and it is not “sanctions always work” — it is a conditional story about design and circumstance.

But — and this is Drezner’s actual headline, in the top callout, so do not lose it — the very same body of research that revised the effectiveness picture upward also revised the costs picture sharply. The policy externalities and unintended consequences of sanctions are far greater than the field once understood. The bottom callout makes the point you must hold: these are two separate corrections moving in opposite directions. We have become less pessimistic about whether sanctions change behavior and more worried about the collateral damage they do. Students love to flatten this into one tidy verdict — “Drezner says sanctions work” or “Drezner says sanctions are bad.” He says both things, about different dimensions, and the tension between them is the whole point.

Smart Sanctions and Their Limits

The policy answer to “sanctions hurt civilians” was targeted (“smart”) sanctions:

  • Hit elites, regime cronies, specific firms — not the whole population
  • Asset freezes, travel bans, blacklisting individual entities
  • The 1990s Iraq experience (mass civilian suffering) drove the shift

But the costs did not vanish — they moved and grew:

  • Financial over-compliance and de-risking strand legitimate trade
  • Humanitarian and third-country spillovers persist

The trade-off Drezner stresses. Targeting made sanctions more usable and more frequent — and because they are now used constantly, the systemic externalities pile up.

“Smart” was a real improvement on blunt embargoes. It was not a free lunch — it lowered the threshold to sanction, so we sanction far more.

One of the most important developments Drezner tracks is the rise of targeted or “smart” sanctions, and you should understand both why they emerged and why they did not solve the problem. The original sin of comprehensive sanctions is that they hammer ordinary people. The defining cautionary tale is Iraq in the 1990s, where a sweeping trade embargo was associated with severe civilian suffering while Saddam Hussein remained in power — a result that looked both cruel and ineffective. That experience drove a deliberate shift toward targeting: instead of embargoing a whole economy, you go after specific individuals and entities — freeze the assets of regime elites and their cronies, ban them from traveling, blacklist particular banks and firms. The theory is surgical: punish the people actually making the decisions, spare the population.

Targeting was a genuine improvement, and Drezner credits it. But notice in the left column that the costs did not disappear; they relocated and, in aggregate, grew. Part of the reason is a phenomenon called over-compliance or de-risking. Faced with the threat of secondary sanctions and huge penalties, banks become so frightened of accidentally touching a sanctioned party that they simply refuse to handle whole categories of legitimate business — they cut off entire countries, charities, or remittance corridors that are perfectly legal, because the risk of getting it wrong is not worth it. So even “smart” sanctions strand legitimate trade and produce humanitarian and third-country spillovers.

And here is the deeper irony, in the top callout, which is really Drezner’s point. By making sanctions look more precise and more humane, targeting made them more usable — it lowered the political threshold for reaching for the tool. So we now sanction constantly, across an ever-growing list of targets. And precisely because sanctions are now used all the time rather than as a rare instrument, the systemic externalities accumulate. Smart sanctions did not give us a free lunch; they gave us a cheaper-feeling lunch that we therefore order far more often, and the bill at the system level keeps climbing. That sets up section five, where overuse comes back to bite the coercer.

Instruments Across Domains

Section 4

Coercion Runs Through Trade, Finance, and Investment

Domain Coercive instruments Flagship example
Trade Export controls; tariffs; import bans; technology denial U.S.-led semiconductor / advanced-chip export controls on China
Finance Financial sanctions; freezing reserves; cutting off payment rails 2022: freezing ~$300B of Russian central-bank reserves
Investment Investment screening; blocking acquisitions; outbound restrictions CFIUS review of foreign acquisitions on national-security grounds

Notice the through-line: every pillar of this course — trade (Ch 2–7), money (Ch 10–13), MNCs and FDI (Ch 8–9) — turns out to have a coercive edge. The economy is the arsenal.

Let us get concrete about the actual instruments, because “economic coercion” can sound abstract until you see the specific levers. And I have organized them by the three domains that map onto the three big units of this course — trade, finance, investment — to make the through-line visible.

Start with trade. The coercive trade tools are export controls, tariffs, import bans, and outright technology denial. The flagship example right now is the U.S.-led effort to restrict China’s access to advanced semiconductors and the equipment used to make them. This is weaponized interdependence in its purest form: the United States and a few allies sit at chokepoints in the chip supply chain — advanced design tools, certain manufacturing equipment, the most cutting-edge fabrication — and they are using control of those chokepoints to deny China the inputs for frontier computing and AI. It is denial power applied to a supply-chain hub. Now finance, which connects straight to the dollar discussion. The headline event is 2022: after Russia invaded Ukraine, the United States and its partners froze roughly three hundred billion dollars of the Russian central bank’s reserves. Sit with how remarkable that is. Russia had deliberately built a war chest of foreign reserves — exactly the self-insurance logic from yesterday’s Asian-crisis lesson — precisely so it would be cushioned against pressure. And in a stroke, the reserves held in Western jurisdictions were frozen. The “safe asset” turned out to be safe only as long as its issuer permitted. That is the weaponized dollar, and it connects directly to the “Sanctions Age” podcast from the monetary unit — the idea that holding your reserves in someone else’s currency means holding them at that someone’s sufferance.

Third, investment, and here I will flag my own research, because this is the area I work in. Coercion runs through investment via screening regimes — governments reviewing and blocking foreign acquisitions on national-security grounds. In the United States that is CFIUS, which we met back in Chapter 9; many other countries have built similar regimes over the past decade. My work on what I call the securitization of political economy is about exactly this: how states have increasingly come to treat ordinary cross-border investment — who can buy what company — as a national-security question, and built new regulatory machinery to police it. The bottom callout is the synthesis I want you to take from this slide: every pillar of the course turns out to have a coercive edge. Trade, money, multinationals, finance — each of them, which we studied as engines of prosperity, is also a potential weapon. The global economy is not just the prize; it is the arsenal.

Limits, Costs, and Blowback

Section 5

Overuse Erodes the Network

The power of a hub depends on everyone wanting to use it. Weaponize it too aggressively and they look for exits:

  • De-dollarization — efforts (so far limited) to settle trade outside the dollar
  • Alternative payment rails — China’s CIPS, other messaging workarounds to route around SWIFT
  • Friend-shoring / decoupling — rebuilding supply chains among trusted partners

Drezner’s “decoupling dilemma.” Aggressive sanctions can erode the very economic governance and network centrality that gave the sanctions their power in the first place.

The weapon has a half-life. Each use is a reason for targets — and nervous bystanders — to build an off-ramp. Coercion is easier to start than to sustain.

This is the section where the story turns on the coercer, and it is the part students find most counterintuitive, so slow down here. The power of a hub is not a permanent endowment. It rests entirely on the fact that everyone wants to use the hub. The dollar is powerful because everyone chooses to invoice, settle, and save in dollars; SWIFT is powerful because everyone routes their messages through it. The moment you start weaponizing a hub aggressively, you give everyone who depends on it — not just your targets, but anxious bystanders watching from the sidelines — a reason to start looking for the exit.

And you can see them looking. De-dollarization is the effort to settle trade outside the dollar — China and Russia doing more trade in their own currencies, talk of alternative reserve arrangements. Be honest with the data: so far this is limited; the dollar’s dominance has proven sticky because the alternatives are worse, and there is no liquid substitute. But the incentive to build one is now permanent. On the payments side, China has built CIPS, its own cross-border interbank payment system, partly as insurance against being SWIFT-ed out the way Iran and Russia were. And on supply chains, the response to chokepoint controls is friend-shoring and decoupling — rebuilding critical supply chains among trusted partners so you are not dependent on a rival’s chokepoint, and so a rival is not dependent on yours in a way you might exploit.

Drezner gives this dynamic a name worth memorizing: the decoupling dilemma. The argument is that aggressive use of sanctions — especially the dollar-based, secondary-sanctions kind — can erode the very economic governance and network centrality that gave the sanctions their power in the first place. Each time the United States cuts someone off from the dollar, it teaches the whole world that dollar access is conditional and political, which strengthens everyone’s incentive to build an alternative. So there is a genuine dilemma: the tool is most powerful when it is rarely used and credible, but the temptation is to use it constantly, and constant use is exactly what corrodes it. The bottom callout is the line to leave with — the weapon has a half-life. Coercion is far easier to start than to sustain, because every use is an advertisement for the off-ramp.

The Politics of Who Coerces Whom

Section 6

Network Power Is Distributed Unequally

Weaponized interdependence has clear winners and losers:

  • Hub states — the U.S. (dollar, SWIFT reach), and increasingly China (manufacturing, critical minerals, CIPS) and the EU (market size, regulatory reach)
  • Everyone else — rule-takers, exposed to chokepoints they don’t control and can’t easily exit
  • Smaller states often face a forced choice between competing great-power systems

The distributional point. “Interdependence” sounds symmetric and mutual. Weaponized interdependence is profoundly asymmetric — it hands leverage to whoever sits at the center.

This is great-power competition by economic means — the contest is over who controls the chokepoints of the next-generation economy.

Step back now and ask the distributional question, because it is where this whole literature connects to the deepest themes of the course. Interdependence, as a word, sounds warm and symmetric — we depend on each other, mutually, equally. Weaponized interdependence is the opposite of symmetric. The power it generates is handed, very unequally, to whoever happens to sit at the center of the network. So there are structural winners and structural losers, and which one you are is mostly determined by your position, not your virtue.

The winners are the hub states. The United States is the paradigmatic one — control of the dollar and reach over SWIFT give it coercive leverage no other single state can match. But it is increasingly not alone. China sits at chokepoints of its own: dominance in manufacturing, in the processing of critical minerals and rare earths, in some supply chains, plus its own payment system in CIPS. The European Union wields a different kind of hub power through the sheer size of its single market and the global reach of its regulations — the so-called Brussels effect. These three are the players who can credibly weaponize interdependence. Almost everyone else is, in the language of the course, a rule-taker — exposed to chokepoints they did not build, cannot control, and cannot cheaply exit. And as the great powers build rival systems, smaller states increasingly face a forced choice: whose payment system, whose technology stack, whose supply chain do you align with, knowing that choosing one may foreclose the other.

So reframe the whole day, in the callouts. This is great-power competition conducted by economic means. The contest between the United States and China is not only, or even mainly, about armies; it is substantially about who controls the chokepoints of the next-generation economy — semiconductors, payments, data, critical minerals, AI. And it is deeply distributional in exactly the sense we have used all semester: the architecture of the global economy is not neutral. It allocates power, it produces winners and losers, and the states at the hubs get to write rules that the states on the periphery have to live with. Weaponized interdependence is, at bottom, another chapter in the politics of who gets what — just played with payment rails and export controls instead of tariffs.

Synthesis & Discussion

Section 7

The Whole Course, Read as an Arsenal

Today’s Big Question — When the global economy is a web of dependencies, who controls the web, and what can they do with it?

What we built

  • Interdependence is not only exposure — it is a resource the well-positioned state can weaponize (Farrell & Newman)
  • Hubs deliver the panopticon (watch) and chokepoint (cut off) effects; the dollar is the master hub
  • Sanctions work better than the old pessimism held — but the externalities are larger, and overuse triggers the decoupling dilemma (Drezner)

Tying it together

  • Trade, money, MNCs, finance — every unit of the course has a coercive edge
  • Network power is asymmetric — it advantages the hub states and leaves others as rule-takers

Discussion: Pick one hub the U.S. controls. Make the strongest case that weaponizing it is a strategic mistake — that it costs more than it buys. Then rebut yourself.

Let me pull the day around the Big Question and, beyond that, around the whole course. The question was: when the global economy is a web of dependencies, who controls the web, and what can they do with it? Farrell and Newman gave us the structural answer — networks have hubs, hubs sit under some state’s jurisdiction, and that state can convert structural centrality into coercive power through the panopticon effect, watching what flows through, and the chokepoint effect, cutting a target off. The dollar system is the master hub, which is why secondary sanctions can conscript the whole world’s banks. Then Drezner gave us the evidence and the cautions: sanctions work better than the old “sanctions never work” consensus admitted, but their unintended costs are far larger than we used to think, “smart” targeting lowered the threshold so we now sanction constantly, and aggressive use triggers the decoupling dilemma — eroding the very network centrality that made the weapon powerful. Easier to start than to sustain.

And here is the move I most want you to make, because it is what ties this day to everything since week one. Go back through the course and re-read each unit as a potential weapon. Trade — the comparative-advantage story of mutual gains — becomes export controls and technology denial. Money — the dollar’s exorbitant privilege — becomes the most powerful financial chokepoint on earth. Multinationals and FDI — the engines of global production we studied in Chapters 8 and 9 — become objects of investment screening and securitized regulation. International finance — the capital flows that fuel and crash developing economies — becomes frozen reserves and severed payment rails. The same global economy we have studied as a machine for producing prosperity is also, read the other way, an arsenal. That double reading is the real takeaway of the day, and arguably of the back half of this course.

For discussion, here is your prompt, in the bottom callout, and I want a real argument, not a summary. Pick one hub the United States controls — the dollar, SWIFT, the chip supply chain. Build the strongest possible case that weaponizing it is a strategic mistake — that the long-run erosion of network centrality, the push toward alternatives, the corrosion of trust, costs the United States more than the immediate coercive win is worth. Make that case as hard as you can. Then turn around and rebut yourself: what is the strongest reply? Maybe the alternatives are decades away and the immediate leverage is decisive; maybe credibility requires occasional use. Sit in both chairs. That tension — between the immediate power of the weapon and its corrosive long-run cost — is the live policy debate happening in Washington, Beijing, and Brussels right now, and you now have the tools to enter it. That is where we will pick up the conversation in class.
