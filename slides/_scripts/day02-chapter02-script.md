## Section: The Leader's Dilemma

### Slide: The Political Logic of Protection

This is the fundamental political logic that drives trade protection everywhere. Spend time here — this slide sets up the entire chapter.

Steel tariffs cost the economy MORE than they save — $5.6 billion in costs versus $3.5 billion in benefits. But the steelworkers have every reason to organize and lobby. Each one stands to gain $25,000. Meanwhile, no individual household is going to fight over $50. This asymmetry — concentrated benefits, diffuse costs — is WHY governments need external rules to resist protectionist pressure.

The key insight: it's individually rational for politicians to protect, even when it's collectively destructive. Ask students: if you're a senator from Pennsylvania, and steel mills are closing, what's your incentive? You don't get rewarded for keeping consumer prices low. You get rewarded for saving visible jobs in your state.

That's the core setup for understanding why the WTO exists.

### Slide: What Happens Without Rules

Smoot-Hawley, 1930. The U.S. raises tariffs on over 20,000 goods. Canada retaliates immediately. Britain abandons free trade in 1932. France and Germany impose quotas. World trade falls 61% in three years — from $2,858 million per month to $1,122 million.

This is the formative experience for the generation that built the postwar trading system. Cordell Hull, who became FDR's Secretary of State, had watched the tariff wars destroy livelihoods in his home district in Tennessee. He spent decades arguing that trade rules were necessary to prevent another collapse. The lesson they all took: without rules to constrain the political temptation to protect, the system unravels — and everyone loses.

That lesson is the origin story of the GATT and ultimately the WTO.

### Slide: The Core Insight

Pause here. This is the framing for the entire chapter.

The WTO is not primarily about economics — it's about solving a political problem. Governments know that open trade is collectively beneficial. But each individual government faces domestic pressure to protect. The institution exists to bind their hands. It's a commitment device.

Draw the analogy: it's like Odysseus having his crew tie him to the mast so he can hear the Sirens without steering the ship into the rocks. Governments tie their own hands through WTO commitments so they can resist the Sirens of protectionist lobbying.

Now let's see how the institution was built and how it works.

## Section: What Is the WTO?

### Slide: The WTO at a Glance

The WTO is remarkably small for what it does. 625 people administering the rules of a $25 trillion global trading system. For context, the World Bank has 10,000 staff and a $2.5 billion budget.

Walk through the three functions: (1) provides a forum for governments to negotiate trade agreements, (2) administers the roughly 60 agreements that already exist, and (3) resolves disputes when governments disagree about whether someone is following the rules.

Key framing: the WTO doesn't make trade policy — it provides the rules of the game. It's a referee, not a player. This matters because critics often blame the WTO for outcomes that are really the result of decisions made by member governments.

### Slide: Two Foundational Principles

These two principles are the foundation of the entire system.

**Market liberalism** is the economic rationale — the claim that open trade is beneficial. This is based on the logic of comparative advantage, which we'll cover in depth in Chapter 3. For now, just note that the WTO rests on the assumption that reducing barriers to trade raises overall welfare.

**Nondiscrimination** is the political architecture. Two specific rules:

- **MFN (Most Favored Nation):** If you give Brazil a lower tariff rate on steel, you must extend that same rate to every WTO member. You can't play favorites. This is powerful because it means every bilateral concession automatically becomes a multilateral one.

- **National treatment:** Once foreign goods clear customs, you can't treat them differently than domestic goods. No separate safety inspections, no discriminatory taxes, no rules that de facto disadvantage imports.

There are exceptions — free-trade areas like USMCA, customs unions like the EU, and the Generalized System of Preferences for developing countries — but the default is equal treatment.

Ask students: why is nondiscrimination so central? Because without it, governments would simply shift from tariffs to targeted discrimination. MFN prevents a race to the bottom of bilateral deal-making.

### Slide: The Bargaining Rounds

Trade rules are created through bargaining rounds. Each round starts at a Ministerial Conference where governments set an agenda, then lower-level officials negotiate the details — sometimes for years — and finally trade ministers conclude the deal.

Walk through the timeline. Two things to notice:

First, the rounds got bigger — from 23 members at the original Geneva Round to 147 at Doha. That reflects the expansion of the system as decolonization brought new members and as countries like China joined.

Second, they got harder. The early rounds focused narrowly on tariff cuts and concluded in a year or two. The Kennedy Round (1960s) brought 35% across-the-board tariff cuts and added antidumping rules. The Tokyo Round (1970s) tackled non-tariff barriers for the first time. The Uruguay Round took eight years and created an entirely new institution — the WTO itself, replacing the provisional GATT.

The Doha Round took 14 years and never reached an overarching agreement. That's not surprising: consensus among 147 governments with very different interests is extraordinarily difficult. That difficulty is one of the defining challenges of the contemporary system, and we'll dig into it shortly.

### Slide: Dispute Settlement

This is the WTO's teeth — arguably its most important innovation.

Under the old GATT, the losing party could block a ruling. Think about that: if the U.S. was found to have an illegal tariff, the U.S. could simply veto the ruling. That made enforcement toothless.

The WTO flipped this with "negative consensus": rulings are automatically adopted unless every single member — including the country that won the case — votes to reject. That's never going to happen. So the system became quasi-judicial.

Walk through the four-step process: consultation (try to work it out bilaterally in 60 days), panel ruling (up to 9 months, three experts examine the case), Appellate Body review (up to 90 days), and then compliance or authorized retaliation.

600-plus disputes have been filed since 1995. Major cases include U.S. steel tariffs (found illegal), the EU's GMO import ban, and the massive Boeing-Airbus subsidy dispute that lasted over 15 years.

But here's the crisis: the U.S. has blocked new Appellate Body appointments since 2017, under both Obama and Trump administrations. The body ceased functioning in December 2019 when it fell below the minimum number of judges. This is the most important institutional crisis in the WTO today. Appeals now go into a void — countries can appeal a ruling, but there's no one to hear the appeal. This effectively allows losing parties to block enforcement, which was exactly the GATT problem the WTO was designed to fix. We'll discuss why the U.S. did this and what it means.

## Section: Who Pays for Open Trade?

### Slide: The Public Goods Problem

Introduce the concept of public goods — goods that are non-excludable (you can't prevent anyone from benefiting) and non-rival (one person's consumption doesn't reduce availability for others). The classic example is a lighthouse: once it's built, every ship benefits, whether or not the ship's owner helped pay for it.

The open trading system has exactly these characteristics. Stable rules, transparent markets, a reliable dispute settlement mechanism — these benefit every country, and you can't exclude countries from benefiting even if they didn't help negotiate the rules or bear the costs of maintaining them.

That creates a free-riding problem. Every government wants the system to exist, but each would prefer someone else to pay the political costs of maintaining it — making domestic concessions, opening markets, enforcing rules against domestic industries. In large groups, the temptation to free ride is overwhelming.

So who pays?

### Slide: Hegemonic Stability Theory

This is one of the most important theoretical frameworks in IPE, and students will encounter it repeatedly throughout the course.

Hegemonic stability theory argues that the free-riding problem is overcome when a hegemon exists — a country so economically dominant that it benefits enough from the open system to pay for it alone, even if others free ride.

Walk through the historical evidence using the chart. British hegemony in the 19th century — when Britain was the world's manufacturing workshop, producing nearly 23% of global output — underpinned the first era of open trade. Britain unilaterally repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, adopted free trade, and maintained an open market even as other countries free-rode on British openness.

As Britain declined and the U.S. rose, there was a transition period where neither country was willing to lead. That's the interwar period: no rules, no leader, catastrophic collapse. This is Kindleberger's famous argument.

After 1945, the U.S. stepped in. By then producing nearly 40% of the world's manufactured goods, the U.S. built the GATT, funded the Marshall Plan, established the Bretton Woods system. American hegemony enabled — and sustained — the postwar trading order.

The theory is clean and powerful. But note the critiques for discussion: does hegemony lead to exploitation rather than openness? Is institutional momentum sufficient to sustain the system without a hegemon?

### Slide: The China Question

This is one of the defining questions in contemporary IPE, and it connects directly to your research agenda.

China's rapid economic growth raises the prospect of a hegemonic transition. Goldman Sachs and other forecasters project China will overtake the U.S. in total GDP at some point, though the timeline keeps shifting. Historical evidence is not reassuring — the transition from British to American hegemony was associated with the catastrophic interwar period.

But there's a counter-argument: the institutions built under American leadership — the WTO, IMF, World Bank — are far stronger than anything that existed in the 1920s. Maybe the WTO can survive a power transition because the institutional infrastructure is resilient enough.

Or maybe not. China has not attempted to lead the WTO-centered system or build a comprehensive alternative — but its policies under Xi Jinping have moved toward greater state direction of the economy, industrial policy, and economic nationalism. The Belt and Road Initiative creates parallel infrastructure. The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) was negotiated without U.S. participation.

Leave this as an open question. It's the backdrop for many of the contemporary challenges we'll discuss throughout the semester.

## Section: The System Under Pressure

### Slide: Why No New Deal Since 1994?

The Doha Round was launched in 2001 with an ambitious agenda: agriculture, services, intellectual property, development. It was supposed to be the "Development Round," reflecting developing countries' frustration that previous rounds had primarily served rich-country interests.

The problem was structural. The round was treated as a "single undertaking" — everything had to be agreed or nothing was. Developed countries wanted liberalization in services markets and stronger intellectual property protections. Developing countries wanted agricultural liberalization and reduced farm subsidies — the EU and U.S. spend billions subsidizing their farmers, which developing-country farmers can't compete against.

Neither side would move first. The 2003 Cancún Ministerial collapsed in dramatic fashion when developing countries, led by the G-20 group including Brazil, India, and China, refused to negotiate on the EU's terms. Multiple attempts to revive the round failed. It effectively ended at the 2015 Nairobi Ministerial, where members couldn't even agree on whether the Doha mandate still existed.

The lesson: with 164 members and a consensus requirement, comprehensive multilateral agreement has become nearly impossible. That structural reality has driven governments to seek alternatives.

### Slide: The Regional Alternative

As the WTO has stalled, governments have turned to regional trade arrangements. The chart tells the story — notice the dramatic acceleration after the Doha Round launched in 2001. As it became clear the multilateral route wasn't producing results, governments pursued bilateral and regional deals instead.

RTAs come in two main forms: free-trade areas (like USMCA) where members eliminate tariffs among themselves but maintain independent external tariffs, and customs unions (like the EU) where members also adopt a common external tariff.

The proliferation raises a fundamental question about the system's coherence. RTAs are inherently discriminatory — they give preferential access to some countries, violating the MFN principle that is supposed to be the system's foundation.

Do RTAs complement the WTO by creating more trade (trade creation)? Or do they fragment the system into rival blocs that divert trade from more efficient producers (trade diversion)? Economists distinguish between these two effects, but nobody knows which predominates empirically. Jacob Viner's original framework from 1950 remains the starting point for this debate.

Point out some major contemporary examples: CPTPP (the Trans-Pacific Partnership without the U.S.), RCEP (the China-centered Asian agreement), the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA). Each reflects a different vision of regional economic integration.

### Slide: Trade, Values, and the WTO's Limits

This connects to the Policy Analysis and Debate section in the chapter. It's a good discussion anchor.

China's treatment of its Uyghur minority includes well-documented forced labor in cotton, apparel, and electronics manufacturing in Xinjiang. The U.S. responded with the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (2021), which creates a rebuttable presumption that all goods from Xinjiang are produced with forced labor and bans their import.

This is inherently discriminatory under WTO rules — you're singling out products from one specific region of one specific country for differential treatment.

The deeper tension: the WTO was built around regulating tariffs and quotas. Its rules were designed for a world where trade disputes were about market access and fair competition. Increasingly, trade policy intersects with human rights, labor standards, environmental protection, national security, and technology competition. The original system wasn't built to handle these questions.

Should trade rules prevent governments from acting on human rights concerns? What are the risks of allowing it — will governments abuse the exception to justify protectionism? This is a genuine dilemma with no easy answer, and it's worth dedicating discussion time to.

## Section: What the WTO Made Possible

### Slide: From Finished Goods to Global Value Chains

Transition to the payoff section. The rules-based trading system didn't just increase the volume of trade. It enabled a fundamental transformation in how goods are produced.

Walk through the chart. 55% of all world trade is now in intermediate goods — parts, components, semi-finished products moving between countries before becoming something a consumer buys. In 1970, that figure was about 30%. The shift is dramatic and ongoing.

The old model: Toyota made cars in Japan and exported finished vehicles. The new model: Toyota sources components from producers in dozens of countries, ships them to assembly plants in 15+ countries, and the finished car might cross borders several times before reaching a consumer.

This is possible because the WTO's rules — nondiscrimination, tariff reduction, dispute settlement — create a stable, predictable environment for firms. If a Japanese auto parts maker can be confident that its shipments to a U.S. assembly plant won't be arbitrarily blocked by tariffs or regulatory changes, it's willing to invest in the specialized equipment needed. If that confidence disappears, the whole system of global production unravels.

### Slide: Nutella: One Product, Seven Countries

Oatley opens the chapter with this example, and it's worth lingering on because it makes the abstract concrete.

One jar of Nutella requires cocoa from Nigeria, hazelnuts from Turkey, sugar from Brazil, vanillin from China, palm oil from Malaysia. These ingredients are transformed in nine factories on four continents. The whole operation is coordinated from a small town in northwestern Italy called Alba.

This isn't some exotic outlier — this is how consumer products work now. Ask students to think about any complex product they use: their phone, their clothes, their car. The production network behind it almost certainly spans multiple countries.

And it's only possible because of stable, predictable trade rules. If Turkey can't be sure its hazelnuts won't face arbitrary tariffs in Europe, if Nigeria's cocoa is subject to unpredictable import restrictions, the whole network breaks down. That's what the WTO protects — not just trade in finished goods, but the complex web of intermediate production that makes modern consumer products possible.

### Slide: How National Economies Have Transformed

The transformation isn't just about individual products — it's about entire national economies.

Walk through the four cases:

- **South Korea** in 1980 exported textiles and wigs. Today it's one of the world's leading semiconductor producers — Samsung and SK Hynix produce about 60% of the world's memory chips.

- **Vietnam** exported crude oil. Now its single largest factory is Samsung's smartphone assembly plant in Thai Nguyen province. Vietnam is the second-largest smartphone exporter in the world.

- **Mexico** was an oil exporter. Now it's deeply integrated into North American auto supply chains. The average car that crosses the U.S.-Mexico border has crossed it multiple times as components are assembled, processed, and assembled again.

- **China** started with light manufactures — toys, garments. Today it produces advanced electronics, telecommunications equipment, and industrial machinery.

The common thread: participation in global value chains, enabled by the stable rules of the WTO system, allowed these countries to move up the value chain. They didn't just trade more — they fundamentally changed what they produce and how they fit into the global economy.

### Slide: ASML's EUV Machine: The Pinnacle

This is the capstone example — the most dramatic illustration of what global production networks have made possible.

ASML's Extreme Ultraviolet lithography system is, by many measures, the most complex machine ever built by humans. It costs $380 million per unit. It weighs 180 tons. It is the ONLY machine in the world that can print the most advanced semiconductor chips — the ones in the latest phones, in AI data centers, in modern cars.

Five thousand suppliers across dozens of countries. Walk through the key components:

- The **mirrors** come from Carl Zeiss in Germany. They must be polished to an accuracy of a fraction of a nanometer — if the mirror were scaled up to the size of Germany, the tallest bump would be 1 millimeter. They are the flattest objects humans have ever made.

- The **light source** comes from Cymer in San Diego. It fires a tiny droplet of molten tin with a laser, creating a plasma at 500,000 degrees Celsius that emits extreme ultraviolet light.

- **Specialty chemicals and optics** from Japan provide the resist materials and precision lenses.

- **Final assembly** happens in Veldhoven, Netherlands — ASML's home base.

- **Customers** are in Taiwan (TSMC) and South Korea (Samsung) — the only companies that can afford and operate these machines.

No single country possesses all the capabilities needed to build this machine. It is the product of decades of specialized innovation across multiple national systems, made possible by the confidence that components would flow freely across borders. This machine is, in a very real sense, a product of the rules-based trading system.

And it's what's at stake if the system fractures. The current U.S. export controls on semiconductor equipment are the most direct test of this — they deliberately restrict the flow of this technology to China. That's a political decision to override the logic of global production networks for national security reasons. We'll return to this throughout the semester.

### Slide: When the System Fractures

The global production network is powerful but fragile. Walk through the disruptions:

- **Thailand floods (2011):** A single flood plain contained much of the world's hard drive manufacturing capacity. When it flooded, global hard drive prices doubled and supply took months to recover.

- **Fukushima (2011):** The earthquake and tsunami disrupted auto supply chains across Japan and globally. GM had to idle plants in Louisiana because a single specialty pigment factory in Fukushima was offline.

- **Suez Canal blockage (2021):** One container ship, the Ever Given, blocked global trade for six days. Estimated cost: $10 billion.

- **Ukraine war (2022):** Disrupted neon gas supplies (Ukraine produced about 50% of the world's semiconductor-grade neon) and agricultural commodity markets.

- **COVID shutdowns (2020):** The big one. Factory closures cascaded across global supply chains. The resulting semiconductor shortage idled auto factories worldwide for over a year. Cars couldn't be built because a tiny chip from Taiwan wasn't available.

This is the frontier of contemporary trade policy debate: the same disaggregated supply chains that make ASML's machine possible also create vulnerabilities. Concentration of production in a few locations means a single disruption can cascade globally. How to balance the efficiency gains of global production networks against the resilience costs of dependence — that's the question policymakers are wrestling with right now. And it connects directly to the research we do on investment screening and supply chain security.

## Section: Discussion

### Slide: In-Class Writing Prompt

Give students 15 minutes for one of the two prompts. No laptops, no LLMs — pen and paper only.

**Prompt A** pushes students to think concretely about supply chains. Most have never thought about where their phone or coffee actually comes from. The exercise of tracing a product forces them to internalize the complexity of global production networks.

**Prompt B** is more analytical and engages directly with the chapter's central argument about the WTO as a commitment device. Look for students who find the argument convincing and those who don't — the skeptics often raise good points about power asymmetries and whose interests the rules actually serve.

For the share-out (10 minutes), call on 3–4 students. Ask follow-up questions that connect their responses back to the chapter's themes: if your phone's supply chain spans 15 countries, what happens when trade rules break down? If the commitment device argument is right, what does it mean that the Appellate Body has stopped functioning?

### Slide: Key Takeaways

Run through the six takeaways quickly — these should reinforce rather than introduce. If students can articulate these six points, they've internalized the chapter's core content.

### Slide: Next Class: Trade Cooperation

Preview Chapter 3. The bridge: if trade benefits everyone in the aggregate, why do governments still fight about it? Chapter 3 gives us the economic theory (comparative advantage, gains from trade) and the political economy framework (who wins, who loses, and why losers are louder than winners).

Remind students to read Chapter 3 before next class.
