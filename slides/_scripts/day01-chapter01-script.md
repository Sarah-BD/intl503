## Defining IPE

### The Domain of IPE
[Diagram: overlapping circles of Politics and Economics with IPE at the intersection]

"Look at this diagram. Most of you have taken either political science courses or economics courses. In those fields, the domains are usually treated separately — markets over here, power over there. IPE sits right in the overlap. The argument is simple: you can't understand one without the other. Markets don't exist in a vacuum — they exist inside political rules. And political power is deeply shaped by economic resources."

Point to the arrows: "Notice the arrows going both ways. Politics shapes economics — tariffs change who gets rich. But economics shapes politics too — wealthy industries buy lobbying power. IPE studies that mutual influence."

### Three Core Questions
[Diagram: three numbered questions]

"Here are the three questions that define the field. First: how do political decisions — like who's in office, what institutions exist — shape who wins and loses economically? Second: how do economic realities — like a country's trade deficit or a firm's market power — shape political outcomes? And third — the one that makes IPE normatively important — how do these interactions affect welfare and distribution? Who gets what?"

Transition: "Now, before those questions seem abstract, let me show you the stakes."

## What Are the Stakes?

### A $117 Trillion System
[Chart: Top 50 economies]

"This is the global economy. $117 trillion. It's 80 times larger than it was in 1960. But look at the distribution — the top 10 economies account for two-thirds of it. All of Africa produces roughly the same GDP as Italy alone. India, with 1.4 billion people, produces roughly what the UK does with 67 million. These disparities aren't natural — they're the product of centuries of political and economic choices."

### Trade: The Engine of Integration
[Chart: trade volume index since 1800]

"Trade is the connective tissue of the global economy. Look at this curve — it's essentially flat for a century, then explodes. Trade today is 2,000 times the 1800 level. That's not just growth; that's a qualitative transformation in how economies relate to each other."

### Trade as Share of the Economy
[Chart: trade/GDP over time]

"But raw volume doesn't tell you how embedded trade is. This chart shows trade as a share of total output. Notice the dip during the wars and Depression — that red shaded area. Globalization isn't a one-way ratchet. It can unravel. It has before. We'll come back to that."

### Who Trades With Whom?
[Chart: trade partners by income level]

"Here's a structural shift that most people miss. In 1990, rich-country trade dominated — the US, Europe, and Japan trading with each other. By 2024, developing-developing trade is as large a share as rich-rich trade. The geography of the global economy is being redrawn."

### China: The New Center of Global Trade
[Chart: China vs. US trade volume]

"And this is the story behind that shift. China overtook the US in total trade volume around 2012. It's now the top import partner for about two-thirds of the world's countries. This happened in barely two decades — one of the fastest redistributions of economic centrality in modern history."

### The Supply Chain Era
[Bar chart + TikZ iPhone supply chain diagram]

"And it's not just about finished products anymore. Over half of all trade is in intermediate goods — parts and components. Your iPhone has parts from 43 countries. A single car crosses borders 8 times during production. This means disruptions anywhere ripple everywhere. When a ship got stuck in the Suez Canal in 2021, it didn't just affect shipping — it affected auto factories in Germany and electronics retailers in the US."

Transition: "So the global economy is enormous, complex, deeply interconnected, and profoundly unequal. The question is: who governs it? Who decides the rules? That's what we need a framework for."

## Model of Analysis

### The IPE Framework
[TikZ diagram: Interests → Institutions → Policy → Outcomes with feedback loop]

"Here's the framework we'll use all semester. It has four steps. First, identify the interests — who gains and who loses from a given policy. Second, trace how those interests get aggregated into political demands — not everyone who's affected organizes equally. Third, analyze the institutions — the rules that determine who gets to make the decision and how. Fourth, explain the policy outcome. And then — crucially — notice the feedback loop. Outcomes reshape interests for the next round."

### The Lumber Tariff: A Case Study
[Callout + winners/losers chart]

"Let's see this in action. April 2017: the Trump administration slaps a 20% tariff on Canadian softwood lumber. Why? The US Lumber Coalition wanted it — domestic mills losing market share. Home builders and consumers opposed it — higher construction costs. Look at the chart. Winners on the right, losers on the left. This is what every trade policy looks like — redistribution."

### Concentrated Benefits, Diffuse Costs
[TikZ diagram: few large arrows vs. many tiny arrows]

"This is the key insight for understanding *why* the mills won. Look at the left side — a few firms, each getting a huge benefit. They have every incentive to organize, lobby, spend money. Now look at the right — millions of consumers, each paying slightly more. No individual consumer is going to organize over a few hundred dollars on their home construction costs. This asymmetry — concentrated benefits, diffuse costs — is one of the most powerful patterns in political economy. You'll see it again and again."

### Four Issue Areas of IPE
[Table]

"The framework applies across four major issue areas that we'll cover this semester. Trade, monetary policy, multinational corporations, and development. Each has its own actors and questions, but the analytical logic is the same: interests, institutions, policy, outcomes."

Transition: "Now, this framework didn't fall from the sky. It grew out of centuries of intellectual debate about what the global economy is *for*."

## Three Ways of Seeing

### Three Intellectual Traditions
[TikZ: three colored columns with key attributes]

"IPE draws on three intellectual traditions, and each one starts from a different premise. Liberalism asks: how does trade serve individual welfare? Nationalism asks: how does trade serve national power? Marxism asks: how does trade distribute wealth between classes? These aren't just academic positions — they map onto real political debates happening right now."

### Adam Smith
[Portrait + key ideas]

"Adam Smith is the foundation of the liberal tradition. Writing in 1776 — the same year as the American Declaration of Independence, not coincidentally — Smith argued that markets coordinate human activity better than any planner could. Trade isn't zero-sum; both sides benefit. The state's job is to protect property rights, not direct production. This is the intellectual foundation of free-trade arguments to this day."

### Friedrich List
[Portrait + key ideas]

"List is the counter-argument. Writing sixty years later, as a German watching Britain dominate the industrial world, List said: of course the British love free trade — they're already ahead. For a country trying to develop, free trade locks you into exporting raw materials while they sell you manufactured goods. Infant industries need protection until they're competitive. This argument — that free trade serves the powerful — has been made by every developing country from Germany to Japan to China."

### Karl Marx
[Portrait + key ideas]

"Marx sees the whole system differently. The question isn't whether trade helps individuals or nations — it's about class. Who owns the means of production? Workers produce the value; capitalists extract the surplus. The state isn't a neutral arbiter — it enforces the interests of capital. And crises aren't accidents; they're built into the system. Whether or not you're a Marxist, the analytical tools — thinking about class, structural power, exploitation — remain indispensable."

### Constructivism
[TikZ: ideas → interests → what seems possible, with feedback loop]

"There's a fourth perspective that cross-cuts these three. Constructivists ask: where do interests come from? The other schools take interests as given — you're a worker or a capitalist, you're in an exporting or import-competing industry. Constructivists say: how you understand your interests depends on ideas. 'Free trade is good' became common sense — but it wasn't always. Someone had to argue for it, and it had to become a taken-for-granted belief before it could shape policy. Ideas aren't just window dressing; they constitute what actors think they want."

### Our Approach
[Callout with formula]

"In this course, we'll draw on all three traditions. Our working equation is: material interests plus political institutions plus ideas about what's possible equals policy outcomes. We won't assume markets always work, or states always seek power, or class conflict explains everything. We'll use whatever lens gives us the most analytical leverage for the case at hand."

Transition: "These ideas didn't develop in a seminar room. They emerged from real crises — and the history matters."

## Rise, Fall, Rebuild, Rupture?

### Two Centuries of Globalization
[TikZ timeline: four eras with trade/GDP line]

"Here's the big picture. Two centuries of globalization in one image. The green era is the first globalization — railroads, steamships, the gold standard. Trade expands dramatically. Then the red era — two world wars and the Great Depression shatter the system. The blue era is reconstruction — Bretton Woods, the GATT, embedded liberalism. Trade recovers and surpasses its earlier peak. And now we may be entering another red period — trade wars, decoupling, institutional crisis. Notice that the line goes down as well as up. Globalization is a political choice, and it can be reversed."

### The First Globalization
[Chart: shipping costs + bullet points]

"Technology made globalization possible — shipping costs dropped 85% in 70 years. But technology alone isn't enough. Britain had to make a political choice to repeal the Corn Laws in 1846. The gold standard required countries to constrain their monetary policy. Trade treaties had to be negotiated and enforced. Technology enables; politics decides."

### The Collapse
[Unemployment chart]

"And when the politics went wrong, the system collapsed catastrophically. Look at Germany — 44% unemployment. The US at 25%. These aren't just numbers; this is the soil in which fascism grew. The lesson the postwar architects took from this period was: if you let the global economy collapse, you don't just get poverty — you get war."

### Bretton Woods
[Table + callout on embedded liberalism]

"So in 1944, while the war was still being fought, delegates from 44 nations gathered in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design a new system. Three institutions: IMF for currency stability, World Bank for development, GATT for trade. But the key insight was what John Ruggie called 'embedded liberalism' — free trade between countries, but policy autonomy within them. Governments could maintain safety nets, regulate their economies, cushion the losers. That bargain held for about 40 years."

### Video
[Bretton Woods footage]

"Let's take a moment to watch the actual footage from Bretton Woods."

### Today's Challenges
[Three-column layout]

"That postwar order is now under unprecedented strain from three directions simultaneously. Populist backlash against globalization — Trump's tariffs, Brexit. Great power rivalry — the US and China weaponizing economic interdependence. And the climate crisis, which demands new forms of governance that the existing institutions weren't designed for."

## Writing Activity

### In-Class Writing
Read the prompt aloud. Emphasize: "This isn't about getting the 'right' answer. I want to see you *use the framework*. Pick a real case, identify the interests, trace the institutions, apply a school. The analytical moves matter more than the polish."

### Share-Out Discussion
Facilitate the pair and class discussion. Listen for common patterns — students often gravitate toward nationalism in their analyses. Note this if it happens: "Interesting that most of you reached for the nationalist lens. Why do you think that is?"
