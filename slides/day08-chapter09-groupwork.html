One Firm, Many Sovereigns

Chile, 1971: Who Owns the Copper?

Chile’s economy ran on copper — and the biggest mines were owned by two U.S. multinationals, Anaconda and Kennecott.

  • In 1971, President Salvador Allende nationalized the mines — a unanimous vote of the Chilean Congress
  • Compensation was slashed for “excess profits”; the firms fought back, lobbied Washington, froze Chilean assets abroad
  • The MNC had become a flashpoint of sovereignty, foreign policy, and Cold War politics

A foreign firm owned a strategic national resource on Chilean soil. Whose rules apply — Chile’s, the home country’s, or the firm’s own global calculation?

That collision is at the heart of the politics of the multinational corporation.

Salvador Allende, President of Chile (1970–1973)

Chapter 8 examined why firms invest abroad. Chapter 9 turns to what happens once they do — when a profit-seeking global firm operates inside a sovereign state with goals of its own.

Let me open where the politics is rawest. For most of the twentieth century, Chile’s economy was copper, and the largest copper mines were owned not by Chileans but by two American multinationals, Anaconda and Kennecott. Think about what that means: the commanding heights of a nation’s economy, its single most important export, owned and run by foreign firms answering to shareholders in New York. In 1971 the socialist government of Salvador Allende nationalized those mines — the Chilean Congress voted unanimously for it — and then, invoking a doctrine of “excess profits,” sharply reduced the compensation paid to the companies. The firms did not go quietly: they pursued legal claims, lobbied the U.S. government, helped freeze Chilean copper shipments and assets abroad, and the episode became entangled with U.S. covert action and the eventual coup against Allende in 1973. I open here because the case strips the chapter down to its essence. A foreign-owned firm controls a strategic asset physically located inside another country’s borders. So whose rules govern it? Chile’s, as the territorial sovereign? The United States’, as the home country protecting its firms? Or the firm’s own global profit calculation, which recognizes no single national master? That collision — between territorial sovereignty and a borderless firm — is the entire subject of today. Yesterday we asked why firms invest abroad. Today we ask what happens politically once they have, when a profit-seeking global enterprise sets up inside a sovereign state that has objectives of its own.

The Core Tension: State and Firm

The State The Multinational
Frame of reference Territorial — one national jurisdiction Global — the worldwide operation
Objective Maximize national welfare (jobs, revenue, security) Maximize firm/shareholder welfare across all markets
Loyalty To citizens who can vote and demand To shareholders, not to any one host
Time horizon Bound to the nation, indefinitely Mobile — can exit, relocate, restructure

Vernon’s insight: “the people in any national jurisdiction have a right to maximize their well-being… The MNC, on the other hand, is bent on maximizing the well-being of its stakeholders from global operations, without accepting responsibility for the consequences in individual jurisdictions.” (Vernon 1998)

The host’s dilemma: it needs MNCs to capture FDI’s benefits, yet cannot trust them to deliver those benefits on their own. Almost all the politics of MNCs is governments managing this dilemma.

Raymond Vernon, the scholar who essentially founded the modern study of multinationals, framed the tension better than anyone, and this table is his diagnosis. The state and the firm operate on two logics that simply do not line up. The state’s frame of reference is territorial — one jurisdiction, bounded by borders. Its objective is to maximize the welfare of the people inside those borders: jobs, tax revenue, security, development. Its loyalty runs to citizens, who vote and organize and make demands. And it is stuck to the nation indefinitely; a government cannot relocate. The multinational’s frame of reference is the entire globe — it optimizes across all its markets at once. Its objective is to maximize returns to its shareholders, wherever they sit. Its loyalty is to those shareholders, not to any particular host country. And, crucially, it is mobile: it can exit, relocate production, restructure its corporate form to route around a rule it dislikes. Vernon’s own words, in the callout, are worth reading aloud: people in a national jurisdiction have a right to try to maximize their well-being within that jurisdiction, while the MNC is bent on maximizing its stakeholders’ well-being from global operations without accepting responsibility for the consequences in any individual jurisdiction. Out of that mismatch comes the host country’s fundamental dilemma, the bottom callout, and it’s the thread for the whole day: the host needs the multinational, because as we saw yesterday FDI brings capital, technology, and access the country can’t easily get otherwise — but it cannot simply trust the firm to deliver those benefits, because the firm is optimizing for itself, not for the host. Nearly everything governments do toward MNCs — regulating, bargaining, incentivizing, and now screening — is an attempt to manage that single dilemma. That dilemma is exactly what your four groups are about to reconstruct, each taking one part of the chapter’s answer: how host countries in the developing world have tried to control multinationals, how advanced economies regulate them, why bargaining power shifts once an investment is sunk, and why the world never built a global rulebook for investment the way it did for trade.

Reference: The Host Country’s Toolkit

A performance requirement is a condition a host government attaches to FDI to make the firm’s behavior serve national objectives — not just the firm’s bottom line.

Tool What it forces the firm to do
Local-content rules A set share of inputs must be sourced domestically — building local suppliers
Export requirements The firm must export a set share of output — earning foreign exchange
Technology transfer The firm must license or share technology with local partners
Joint-venture / equity caps Foreign ownership is capped; a local partner must hold equity (control stays partly home)
Employment & training Mandated hiring, training, or management localization of host-country nationals

Reference table only — the performance-requirement toolkit (local-content rules, export requirements, technology transfer, JV/equity caps, employment & training). Useful for Group 1. The aim is to capture the spillovers from Chapter 8: rather than let the MNC simply assemble and repatriate profits, these rules require linkages to local firms, workers, and technology. Several of these tools were later restricted by the WTO’s TRIMs agreement as trade-distorting — a link Group 4 may surface too.

Reference: Nationalization and Export-Processing Zones

Export-processing zones (EPZs) — carved-out enclaves offering duty-free imports, tax breaks, streamlined rules, and (often) lighter labor regulation, in exchange for export-oriented jobs. Taiwan and South Korea used EPZs from the 1960s–70s to attract investment even while regulating it closely elsewhere; from a handful of zones in the 1970s to thousands worldwide today.

Reference chart + EPZ definition. Useful for Group 1. The chart shows the nationalization wave (Fig. 9.1 in Oatley) rising through the NIEO era, peaking around 1975, then collapsing by the early 1980s as the debt crisis left developing countries needing capital and bargaining power began shifting back toward firms — the mechanism Group 3 will explain. EPZs are the other side of the developing-world story: even the most restrictive regulators, like Taiwan and South Korea, built EPZs specifically to attract the investment they wanted.

Reference: Sovereign Wealth Funds

A sovereign wealth fund (SWF) is a state-owned investment fund that invests national savings — often oil revenue or reserves — in assets abroad.

Reference chart only. Useful for Group 2. SWFs collectively manage on the order of $13 trillion (2025); Norway’s fund alone holds about $1.8 trillion; the other major players are China, the Gulf states, and Singapore. This chart doesn’t cover why advanced economies restrict MNC ownership of sensitive sectors (Japan’s historical restrictions, the CNOOC-Unocal 2005 and Dubai Ports World 2006 blocked deals, Lenovo-IBM’s 2005 approval after scrutiny) — that’s in the reading (pp. 192–196) and Group 2 should pull those cases directly from the text. When the investor is a foreign government rather than a private firm, hosts suspect political rather than commercial motives — that’s the hinge into the national-security screening we close on today.

Reference: The Obsolescing Bargain

Reference chart only. Useful for Group 3. Before investing, the firm can shop among eager hosts and win generous terms. After the capital is sunk into an immovable mine, plant, or pipeline, it can’t credibly threaten to leave, so the host can raise taxes, demand renegotiation, even expropriate — the original deal obsolesces (Moran 1974). This is the single most useful concept in the chapter: it explains both the expropriation wave Group 1 covers and why firms came to demand the treaty protections Group 4 covers. Bargaining power differs by sector — natural-resource investments are highly susceptible to this dynamic; low-skilled manufacturing, where capital is mobile and no host has a monopoly on cheap labor, is not, which is why MNCs retain more leverage there.

Reference: Locational Incentives

Inducements a host offers to win an investment: tax holidays, subsidies, cash grants, cheap land, infrastructure, regulatory relief.

Example — the U.S. South: in 1990 the Deep South built no cars; today Alabama, Georgia, Mississippi, and South Carolina build 2M+/year, lured by competing state incentive packages. BMW’s Spartanburg, SC plant (1992) started it — roughly $135M in incentives, about $67,500 per job. Alabama’s Mercedes package (mid-1990s) ran $253–300M, roughly $200,000–250,000 per job.

Reference text only. Useful for Group 3. The growing size and frequency of these packages — across the OECD, from 285 programs totaling $11B in 1989 to 362 programs totaling $18B by 1993 — suggests host countries are at a disadvantage bargaining with MNCs over manufacturing investment specifically, the flip side of the obsolescing bargain: manufacturing capital is more mobile than natural-resource capital, so hosts compete rather than extract. Worth connecting explicitly to the obsolescing-bargain chart on the previous reference slide.

Reference: Asserting Sovereignty — The Right to Nationalize

When performance requirements weren’t enough, developing host countries asserted a more radical claim in the 1960s–70s: the sovereign right to nationalize foreign firms.

The Calvo Doctrine

A foreign investor is entitled to no more than national treatment, and disputes must be settled in the host’s own courts under host law — not through the investor’s home government or international arbitration. Sovereignty, not special protection.

Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources

UN General Assembly Resolution 1803 (1962) affirmed every state’s right to control — and expropriate — foreign-owned resources in the national interest, with compensation set by the host. The economic charter of the NIEO.

Reference text only. Useful for Group 4. Both doctrines assert the same principle: the territorial state is supreme over the foreign firm. This marks the peak of host-country power — and it is what the later BIT/ISDS treaty regime was designed to undo. The Calvo Doctrine, named for the nineteenth-century Argentine jurist Carlos Calvo, holds that a foreign investor may not appeal to their home government or international arbitration; the 1962 UN Resolution globalized that principle for natural resources specifically. Group 4 should connect this to where the chapter ends up: the BIT regime is essentially the negation of Calvo, since it lets firms bypass host courts entirely and sue states before international arbitrators.

Reference: Trade Has the WTO; Investment Has 3,300 Treaties

Reference chart only. Useful for Group 4. A bilateral investment treaty (BIT) is a two-country pact protecting each other’s investors — national treatment, MFN, limits on expropriation, and investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS): the firm can sue the state before international arbitrators. Instead of one organization, the world built 3,300 of these, one at a time, because capital-exporting and capital-importing states could never agree in a multilateral venue. Group 4 has two more reference slides on this — the TRIMs/MAI failures and the home-vs-host conflict table — use whichever combination best supports their explanation.

Reference: TRIMs and the MAI

Governments spent decades trying to build multilateral investment rules, with limited success.

TRIMs — a partial measure

The WTO’s Trade-Related Investment Measures agreement (1995) bans only the trade-distorting performance requirements — chiefly local-content and trade-balancing rules. It disciplines a few host tools but creates no general investment regime.

MAI — the comprehensive attempt

The Multilateral Agreement on Investment, negotiated at the OECD (1995–1998), aimed to be a comprehensive investment treaty — strong investor protections, binding ISDS. It collapsed in 1998 amid NGO and public opposition over sovereignty, and disagreement among the negotiating governments themselves.

Earlier attempts also failed: the 1948 Havana Charter (ITO) and the 1970s UN Code of Conduct on TNCs. Across three venues — UN, GATT/WTO, OECD — and nearly 30 years of talks, no comprehensive framework emerged.

Reference material only. Useful for Group 4. TRIMs came in with the WTO’s founding in 1995 and bans only local-content and trade-balancing rules — a trade agreement that clips a few investment-related wings, not a general regime. The MAI, negotiated among the rich countries at the OECD from 1995–1998, was meant to be the real thing — a comprehensive treaty with binding investor-state arbitration — and collapsed under a combination of NGO/public opposition and disagreement among OECD governments themselves (France ultimately walked away). Three venues, nearly three decades, no comprehensive multilateral framework.

Reference: Why No WTO for Investment? Home vs. Host

Home countries Host countries
Who they are Capital-EXPORTERS — advanced, home to MNCs Capital-IMPORTERS — developing, host to MNCs
What they want rules to do PROTECT investors; lock in access; restrain host interference PRESERVE sovereignty; keep the right to regulate & screen
Their model doctrine Strong investor rights + ISDS (anti-Calvo) Calvo Doctrine + permanent sovereignty
Whom rules should bind Discipline HOST STATES Discipline the FIRMS

Reference table only. Useful for Group 4. Rules that protect firms (what home countries want) are rules that constrain host sovereignty (what host countries refuse) — the same rule, read from two directions. With no overlap on whom to bind, the multilateral deal never closed, and the world defaulted to a decentralized, investor-protective BIT patchwork instead. Contrast the WTO (state-to-state; only states have standing; one organization) with the investment “non-regime” (investor-to-state ISDS; firms sue states; thousands of treaties, no organization) — that contrast is the direct answer to today’s Big Question.

The Securitization of Investment

Emerging issue

From Attracting FDI to Screening It

The pattern reverses. For four decades, states competed to attract FDI; now advanced economies screen and sometimes block it on national-security grounds. In the U.S., CFIUS — strengthened by FIRRMA (2018) — reviews foreign deals; 342 filings in 2023 alone. “Economic security is national security.”

🎧 Podcast: Skadden, Economic Statecraft & Foreign Investment Screening — the screening “toolkit” in practice.

And now the emerging issue we’ve been building toward, and notice it completes a full circle back to the beginning of the lecture and back to the four groups’ reports. For roughly forty years, the dominant story your groups just reconstructed was liberalization and competition to attract FDI — Group 3’s locational incentives, EPZs from Group 1’s reading, the BIT explosion Group 4 covered. The chart shows the counter-movement that has erupted in the last decade: the spread of national-security investment screening, creeping along at a low level through the 2000s, then bending sharply upward after 2018’s FIRRMA reform, which substantially expanded CFIUS, the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States. CFIUS is busy — 342 filings in 2023 alone — and even investment from a close ally can now be blocked. The animating slogan, which you’ll hear in the Skadden podcast, is “economic security is national security.” Here’s the conceptual payoff: the developing host countries of the 1970s asserted sovereignty over foreign firms through the Calvo Doctrine and expropriation (Group 4’s reading); the advanced capital-exporters spent forty years dismantling that and building investor protections; and now those same advanced economies are reasserting exactly that host sovereignty — the right to regulate, screen, and block foreign investment — but rebranded from “economic nationalism” to “national security.” In effect, the Calvo impulse is returning in the world’s richest countries, now justified on security grounds. That’s our reading and today’s student presentation.

Recap: Who Governs the Multinational?

Today’s Big Question — A multinational answers to many governments but to no global authority — so who actually controls it?

From control to competition

  • One firm, many sovereigns: Vernon’s tension — global firm vs. territorial state
  • Group 1: hosts push back — performance requirements, nationalization, then EPZs to attract investment
  • Group 2: advanced economies regulate too — sector restrictions, sovereign wealth funds
  • Group 3: the obsolescing bargain explains the swing — power shifts once capital is sunk, and firms compete for it via locational incentives

Governance, or its absence

  • Group 4: no World Investment Organization — the Calvo Doctrine, TRIMs, the MAI’s collapse — home and host want opposite rules
  • So: 3,300 BITs + ISDS, not one body
  • Now: screening & CFIUS rebuild host control as national security (securitized political economy)

The throughline: the multinational has long operated in the gap between a global economy and a world of sovereign states. No international organization filled that gap, and today the security state is moving into it.

Let’s pull the whole day together around the Big Question — what I lectured, and what your four groups just built and reported back. The left column traces host countries moving from controlling MNCs to competing for them, and it’s entirely your groups’ work: Group 1 traced how developing hosts pushed back with performance requirements and nationalization, then built EPZs to attract investment once the expropriation wave collapsed; Group 2 showed that even advanced economies regulate MNCs, especially through sovereign wealth fund scrutiny; Group 3 gave us the single most useful concept in the chapter, the obsolescing bargain, which explains why power swings the way it does and why hosts compete for mobile manufacturing investment with locational incentives. The right column is Group 4’s story, and it’s the one to remember: investment never got an organization the way trade got the WTO, because home and host countries wanted rules to do opposite things — protect firms versus preserve sovereignty — and could never agree whether to bind states or bind firms. So the world defaulted to a decentralized patchwork of 3,300 bilateral treaties with investor-state arbitration. And now the newest turn, which I lectured directly: the securitization of investment, screening regimes and CFIUS rebuilding host control over the foreign firm, but in the language of national security rather than economic nationalism. The throughline, the closing callout: the multinational corporation has always lived in the gap between a genuinely global economy and a world still organized into sovereign states. No international organization was ever built to fill that gap — and today it is the national-security state that is moving in to fill it. That’s the politics of the multinational corporation.
