The Course Spine

Section 2

The One Move the Whole Exam Rewards

Almost every unit was built as a paired contrast. The integrative skill is to:

  1. Name the phenomenon (a tariff, an FDI screen, a peg, a bailout)
  2. Explain it society-centered — domestic groups, who wins and loses, distributive politics
  3. Explain it state-centered — the national interest, power, security, strategic goals
  4. Say where the two converge and where they diverge

Society-centered: politics is the sum of domestic interests competing over policy. Outcomes reflect who organizes and who wins.

State-centered: the state has goals of its own — power, security, autonomy, strategic position — that markets and interest groups don’t capture.

This is the most important slide in the deck. The course is organized as a series of paired lenses. In trade we did a society-centered chapter and then a state-centered chapter. In money we did exactly the same thing. The exam is testing whether that pairing became a reflex. So when you hit any question, run the move: name the thing, explain it as the product of competing domestic interests — who gains, who loses, who organizes — and then explain it as something a state does in pursuit of power, security, or strategic advantage that no interest-group story fully captures. Then the part students forget: say where they agree and where they part ways. Often both lenses predict the same policy for different reasons; sometimes they flatly disagree, and the disagreement is the interesting part. Do that and you are doing IPE.

Unit 1 — Trade (Chs 1–5)

Section 3

Trade: Key Terms & Frameworks

Term What to know
Comparative advantage Mutual gains from specialization — the economic case for free trade
The WTO / trade regime Reciprocity, non-discrimination (MFN), and dispute settlement to sustain cooperation
Stolper-Samuelson (factor model) Society-centered: trade divides by factor — abundant factors win, scarce factors lose
Ricardo-Viner (sector model) Society-centered: trade divides by industry — export sectors win, import-competing lose
Strategic trade / industrial policy State-centered: governments shape comparative advantage via rent-shifting and infant-industry support

The paired contrast. Society-centered asks which groups win and lose (factor vs. sector); state-centered asks what the nation can strategically gain. Same tariff, two stories.

Unit one is trade. The economic backbone is comparative advantage — the reason free trade produces mutual gains — and the puzzle that runs through the unit is why something so beneficial in the aggregate is so hard politically. The WTO chapter answers part of that: reciprocity, most-favored-nation non-discrimination, and dispute settlement are the institutional machinery that lets states cooperate despite the temptation to defect. Then the two society-centered models, which students mix up, so keep them straight. Stolper-Samuelson is the factor model — trade splits society along factor lines, the abundant factor gains and the scarce factor loses, so it predicts broad class-like coalitions. Ricardo-Viner is the specific-factors or sector model — factors are stuck in industries, so the cleavage is export industries versus import-competing industries. Which model fits depends on how mobile factors are. The state-centered chapter flips the question: instead of who wins domestically, it asks what the nation can strategically engineer — rent-shifting in concentrated industries, infant-industry support, modern industrial policy in chips and jets. Same tariff, two stories.

Unit 2 — Development & MNCs (Chs 6–9)

Section 4

Development & MNCs: Key Terms & Frameworks

Term What to know
Import-substitution industrialization (ISI) State-led development behind protective barriers to build domestic industry
Neoliberalism / structural adjustment Market-oriented turn: liberalize, privatize, deregulate — often via conditionality
Foreign direct investment (FDI) A foreign firm builds or controls productive assets — the sticky form of capital
The obsolescing bargain Bargaining power shifts to the host state once the investment is sunk
Investment screening (e.g., CFIUS) Society- and state-centered both: regulating inward FDI on security grounds

The development debate is the paired contrast again. ISI is the statist development model; neoliberalism is its market-oriented rival. The 1980s debt crisis was the lever that swung the developing world from one to the other.

Unit two runs from development strategy into multinationals. The big comparison is ISI versus neoliberalism — state-led industrialization behind tariff walls versus the market-oriented turn toward liberalizing, privatizing, and deregulating. Know the case for each and why the pendulum swung: the 1980s debt crisis, which we will revisit in unit four, handed the IMF and World Bank the leverage to push neoliberal conditionality and dismantle ISI. Then MNCs and FDI. FDI is the sticky, hard-to-pull-out form of foreign capital — contrast it with the hot money that causes crises. The obsolescing bargain is the key strategic concept: before a firm invests, the host courts it; once the capital is sunk in a factory that cannot move, bargaining power flips to the state, which can then tax or regulate more aggressively. And the politics-of-MNCs chapter — built on my own EJIR article — is about how states regulate inward investment on security grounds, with CFIUS-style screening as the example, which is both a domestic-interest story and a state-security story at once.

Unit 3 — Money (Chs 10–13)

Section 5

Money: The Trilemma Is the Spine

The trilemma (unholy trinity). A country can have at most two of: a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and an independent monetary policy — never all three.

The three historical regimes are three different choices about which corner to give up:

  • Gold standard: fixed + open → sacrifice domestic autonomy
  • Bretton Woods: fixed + autonomy → restrict capital
  • Floating: autonomy + open → give up the fixed rate

Society-centered (Ch 12): exchange-rate and monetary preferences trace to who is exposed — tradables vs. non-tradables, debtors vs. creditors. The Phillips curve framed the inflation-employment trade-off.

State-centered (Ch 13): the time-consistency problem and the case for central-bank independence as a credible commitment to low inflation.

Unit three is money, and the trilemma is the spine — if you remember one thing from the monetary weeks, make it this. The unholy trinity says you cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent domestic monetary policy; pick two, lose one. That single constraint organizes the whole history. The gold standard fixed the rate and kept capital open, so it sacrificed domestic policy autonomy. Bretton Woods wanted fixed rates plus the autonomy to run full-employment policy, so it restricted capital flows. The floating era keeps autonomy and open capital, so it lets the rate move. The balance of payments is the accounting that sits underneath all of it. Then the same society-versus-state pairing we keep seeing: the society-centered chapter explains monetary and exchange-rate preferences by exposure — tradables versus non-tradables sectors, debtors versus creditors, with the Phillips curve as the inflation-employment trade-off; the state-centered chapter is about time-consistency, why governments are tempted to over-inflate, and central-bank independence as the institutional fix that makes low-inflation promises credible. That credible-commitment logic comes straight back in unit four on the borrower’s side.

Unit 4 — Finance & Coercion (Chs 14–15)

Section 6

Developing-Country Finance & Crisis

Term What to know
The savings gap Poor countries can't self-finance investment — the case FOR foreign capital
Sovereign debt as a hold-up problem No one can force a state to repay, so the developmentally useful capital may never flow
Liquidity vs. solvency Reschedule a temporary cash squeeze; write down a debt that can never be paid
The capital-flow cycle / sudden stop Capital arrives in surges and exits all at once — the volatile forms cause crises
The IMF (conditionality) Crisis lender, not development bank; loans tied to required policy reforms

Two episodes, one spine: surge → buildup → sudden stop → crisis → IMF. Latin America (1980s) = sovereign bank debt + a U.S. rate shock. Asia (1997) = short-term capital + currency mismatch + contagion.

Unit four has two halves. First, developing-country finance, which we did as a combined session. The whole arc hangs on one spine: foreign capital surges in, vulnerability builds, the flow suddenly stops, crisis follows, and the IMF arrives. The savings gap is the case for foreign capital in the first place. Sovereign debt is a hold-up problem because no court can force a state to repay — the same credible-commitment logic from the monetary unit, now on the borrower’s side. The single most useful diagnostic is liquidity versus solvency: a temporary cash squeeze gets rescheduled, a genuinely unpayable debt needs a write-down, and misdiagnosing the two is how the 1980s lost decade dragged on. The capital-flow cycle is the boom-and-bust pattern, and the volatile, reversible forms of capital are what do the damage. The IMF is a crisis lender, not a development bank, and conditionality is its core tool. Then hold the comparison: Latin America in the 1980s was sovereign bank debt triggered by the Volcker shock; Asia in 1997 was short-term private capital with a currency mismatch, ending in contagion. Same spine, different vertebrae.

Economic Coercion & Weaponized Interdependence

The Day 14 move: the same interdependence that creates crisis vulnerability can be deliberately weaponized by states.

  • Weaponized interdependence — turning network ties into instruments of coercion (Farrell & Newman)
  • Panopticon effect — using network hubs to gather information
  • Chokepoint effect — using hubs to cut adversaries off
  • Sanctions — the policy tool; effects, limits, and who actually bears the cost (Drezner)

The turn that closes the course. Earlier units asked how interdependence makes states vulnerable. This one asks how states use that same interdependence as a weapon.

This is state-centered IPE in its purest form: economic networks as instruments of power and security, not just efficiency.

The second half of unit four is the coercion day, which is the conceptual capstone. The earlier units mostly treated interdependence as something that happens to states and makes them vulnerable — to crises, to capital flight. Farrell and Newman flip it: states with privileged positions in the network — control over financial messaging, key currencies, critical nodes — can weaponize that interdependence. Two mechanisms: the panopticon effect, using a hub to see what others are doing, and the chokepoint effect, using a hub to deny access. Sanctions are the applied policy tool, and the Drezner review is honest about how often they actually work and who bears the cost. For the exam, file this as the purest state-centered story we did: economic networks as instruments of power and security, not merely engines of efficiency. It is also a natural hinge for the policy question, because contemporary scenarios — sanctions, export controls, decoupling — sit right here.

How to Answer the Policy Question

Section 7

A Rubric for Section III

Step What to do
1 · Define State precisely what the scenario is and which concepts are in play
2 · Apply a framework Run the society-centered AND state-centered lenses — and use more than one
3 · Bring evidence Anchor each claim with a case or finding from the course (a crisis, an article, a chapter)
4 · Address trade-offs Name what each framework gets right and what it misses; show the tension
5 · Take a stance Stake out a defensible interpretation — don't just list views, choose and justify one

Sample prompt. “A major economy imposes investment screening and export controls on a rival, citing national security. Using the frameworks of this course, analyze the politics driving this policy and assess whether it will achieve its aims.”

This is the slide to study for the essay. The applied policy question gives you a contemporary scenario and asks you to analyze it with course frameworks and stake out a defensible interpretation. The rubric is the same shape every time. First, define — say clearly what the scenario is and which concepts apply; do not rush past this. Second, apply a framework, and bring more than one to bear; the prompt rewards exactly the society-versus-state move from section two. Third, evidence — every analytical claim should be anchored to something concrete from the course: a crisis episode, an article finding, a chapter argument. Fourth, address trade-offs — show what each lens illuminates and what it misses, because the tension is where the analysis lives. Fifth, take a stance; do not end with on-the-one-hand-on-the-other — choose an interpretation and defend it. The sample prompt is the kind of thing you might see: it sits at the intersection of the MNC-screening material, industrial policy, and weaponized interdependence, so it lets you run several frameworks at once. That is exactly what the essay is testing.
