The Central Analytic Move

Section 2

The One Comparison Every Section Rewards

Almost every unit was built as a paired contrast. The move to practice:

  1. Name the phenomenon (a tariff, an FDI screen, a peg, a bailout)
  2. Explain it society-centered
  3. Explain it state-centered
  4. Say where the two converge and where they diverge
A phenomenon
(tariff, peg, bailout…)
→
Society-centered
who wins, who loses
State-centered
power, security, strategy
→
Converge, or diverge?

Society-centered asks who organizes and who wins domestically. State-centered asks what the nation gains in power, security, or strategic position. Most exam questions reward running both.

This slide matters more than any other in the deck. The course is organized as a series of paired lenses — in trade we did a society-centered chapter and then a state-centered chapter, and money repeated the same pairing. The exam is testing whether that pairing became a reflex. So on any question, run the move: name the thing, explain it as the product of competing domestic interests — who gains, who loses, who organizes — then explain it as something a state does in pursuit of power, security, or strategic advantage that no interest-group story fully captures. Then the part students forget: say where the two lenses agree and where they part ways. Often both predict the same policy for different reasons; sometimes they flatly disagree, and the disagreement is the interesting part.

Use the diagram to walk through one example live — a tariff, a currency peg, whatever’s fresh — and have the class call out the society-centered story, then the state-centered one, then whether they converge.

Quick-Recall Review

Section 3

Try It First: Trade & Development

Before you look at the next slide — without notes, name: the two competing society-centered models of trade politics, the state-centered alternative to both, and what changed between ISI and neoliberalism. Then check yourself.

Unit 1 · Trade (Chs 1–5)

Term In short
Comparative advantage mutual gains from specialization
WTO / trade regime reciprocity, MFN, dispute settlement
Stolper-Samuelson trade divides by factor
Ricardo-Viner trade divides by sector
Strategic trade state shapes advantage via policy

Unit 2 · Development & MNCs (Chs 6–9)

Term In short
ISI state-led industrialization behind tariffs
Neoliberalism liberalize, privatize, deregulate
FDI sticky capital; firm controls the asset
Obsolescing bargain power shifts to host once sunk
Investment screening security review of inward FDI

Give this a minute of real silence before revealing anything — the point is retrieval, not recognition. Stolper-Samuelson is the factor model, abundant factor wins and scarce factor loses; Ricardo-Viner is the sector model, export industries versus import-competing ones. Strategic trade is the state-centered answer to both: governments shaping comparative advantage on purpose. For development, the throughline is the pendulum from ISI to neoliberalism, swung by the 1980s debt crisis we covered in Chapter 14. FDI is the sticky, hard-to-pull form of capital; the obsolescing bargain explains why host-state leverage grows once a factory is built and can’t move; investment screening is both a domestic-interest story and a state-security story at once.

Try It First: Money & Finance

Before you look at the next slide — without notes, state the trilemma, name which corner each historical regime gave up, and explain the difference between a liquidity crisis and a solvency crisis. Then check yourself.

Unit 3 · Money (Chs 10–13)

Term In short
Trilemma fixed rate, open capital, autonomy — pick two
Gold standard fixed + open → gave up autonomy
Bretton Woods fixed + autonomy → gave up open capital
Floating era autonomy + open → gave up the fixed rate

Unit 4 · Finance & Coercion (Chs 14–15)

Term In short
Sovereign debt a hold-up problem — no court can force repayment
Liquidity vs. solvency reschedule vs. write down
Capital-flow cycle surge, sudden stop, crisis
Weaponized interdependence network hubs as leverage
Sanctions do they work, and who pays

Same retrieval-first structure. The trilemma is the organizing constraint for the entire monetary unit: fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, independent monetary policy — pick two, lose one. Gold standard gave up autonomy, Bretton Woods gave up open capital, floating gives up the fixed rate. Central-bank independence is the state-centered fix for the time-consistency problem.

For unit four, the pattern is the same across both crisis waves: a surge of capital, a vulnerability that builds quietly, a sudden stop, a crisis, and the IMF. Liquidity versus solvency is the single most useful diagnostic — mistaking one for the other is how the 1980s dragged on. Then the turn to coercion: the same network ties that create crisis vulnerability can be deliberately used as leverage by a state that controls a hub. That’s the purest state-centered material in the course — economic networks as instruments of power and security, not just efficiency.

Cracking the Policy Question

Section 4

Try It Yourself, First

Sample prompt. “A major economy imposes investment screening and export controls on a rival, citing national security. Using the frameworks of this course, analyze the politics driving this policy and assess whether it will achieve its aims.”

Take five minutes and sketch an actual outline — not just a mental note. Which concepts apply? What’s the society-centered story? The state-centered one? Where do they converge or diverge? Only then go to the next slide.

Hold the line here — don’t advance until people have actually written something down, even a rough bullet list. The value of this exercise depends entirely on attempting a real answer before seeing the rubric, live or solo. If you’re running this synchronously, give the full five minutes in silence, or open the chat for people to post their opening line.

This prompt sits at the intersection of the MNC-screening material, industrial policy, and weaponized interdependence, so it rewards running several frameworks at once — which is exactly why it’s a good model for what Section III will ask.

Check Your Answer Against the Rubric

Step Check yourself
1 · Define Did you state precisely what the scenario is and which concepts apply?
2 · Apply a framework Did you run both the society-centered AND state-centered lenses?
3 · Bring evidence Did you anchor each claim in a specific case, article, or chapter?
4 · Address trade-offs Did you name what each framework gets right and what it misses?
5 · Take a stance Did you choose a defensible interpretation, rather than just listing views?

Now compare what you sketched against this rubric, step by step, rather than me just reading it to you. Most incomplete answers stall at step two — running only one lens — or skip step five and never commit to a stance. If your five-minute sketch is missing a step, that’s exactly the thing to build into your Section III habits before the exam starts. This shape repeats for every prompt in Section III, so the rubric is worth internalizing once, here, rather than re-deriving it under time pressure.
