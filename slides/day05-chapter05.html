The Limits of Society-Centered Politics

Boeing vs. Bombardier, or U.S. vs. Canada?

2017–18: Boeing vs. Bombardier. Boeing asks Washington to impose ~300% tariffs on Bombardier’s new C-Series jet — not because Boeing is an import-competing loser, but because Canada subsidized a rival in a market both want to dominate.

  • Not import-competing industry vs. foreign exporter
  • Two export-oriented firms fighting over global market share
  • Tariffs as retaliation over a subsidy rather than proactive protection

Boeing’s trade complaint had the opposite of its intended effect: pressured by the dispute, Bombardier sold the C-Series to Airbus — exchanging a midsize competitor for a far stronger one. (The jet now flies as the Airbus A220.)

The society-centered model asks which lobby won. Here both firms are powerful exporters, and the contest is over which country’s firm captures the industry’s rents.

Rents = returns above what the same capital and labor would earn in their next-best use — the excess profits of a protected market position.

To explain this conflict we need a theory in which the state has goals of its own (not just an aggregator of societal interests) — and in which protection can sometimes make a country richer.

Start with Oatley’s opening puzzle because it does the work of motivating the whole chapter. In 2016 Delta ordered 135 of Bombardier’s new C-Series jets. Boeing, which did not even make a directly competing aircraft in that size class, filed a complaint and the Commerce Department announced tariffs of roughly three hundred percent, alleging that Bombardier had dumped the jets below cost because the Canadian government had handed it about 1.6 billion dollars of development support. Now ask the society-centered question from last week: which import-competing industry organized and captured policy? The question doesn’t fit. This is not a domestic import-competing industry against a foreign exporter. It is two export-oriented firms — Boeing and Bombardier — battling over global market share, and the instrument is not a protective tariff but retaliation against a foreign subsidy. The conflict is about who gets to capture the profits of a concentrated global industry. The society-centered framework, which treats the state as a register of interest-group pressure and treats protection as pure welfare loss, simply cannot narrate this. We need two new ideas: a state with objectives independent of any single lobby, and the possibility — heretical after Chapter 3 — that intervention can sometimes raise national income rather than lower it. The ITC, incidentally, ruled against Boeing in 2018 and the tariffs were never imposed — but there is a further twist, shown in the photo on the slide. Under pressure from the dispute, Bombardier had already sold its C-Series program to Airbus, which incorporated the jet into its own lineup as the A220. Boeing’s attempt to disadvantage a smaller Canadian entrant ultimately transferred the aircraft to its single most capable rival — a self-inflicted strategic setback that no society-centered lobbying account would have predicted. The state-centered reading is almost immediate; the point for today is simply that last week’s model cannot narrate the conflict at all.

The State-Centered Approach: Two Core Claims

Society-Centered (Ch. 4) State-Centered (Ch. 5)
1 · Protection can RAISE welfare Protection always destroys welfare — it forfeits the gains from trade and traps resources in losing industries. Under specific conditions (scale, learning, rents), targeted intervention can move resources where the market won't, increasing country-level aggregate welfare.
2 · The state can act ON ITS OWN Policy is the outgrowth of interest-group pressure. The state has no goals of its own. Under specific conditions, insulated policymakers pursue a *national* objective that no lobby asked for.

Both claims must hold jointly:

  1. If protection cannot raise welfare, intervention is pointless.
  2. If the state cannot act autonomously, the account reduces to society-centered lobbying.

The state-centered approach rests on two assumptions, and each one directly contradicts a pillar of last week’s model. The first is a claim about welfare. The society-centered approach inherited Chapter 3’s verdict that protection is dead-weight loss — it forfeits the gains from trade and keeps resources stuck in comparatively disadvantaged industries. The state-centered approach says: not always. Under a specific and limited set of conditions — economies of scale, learning effects, oligopoly rents — there are industries the market won’t move resources into even though society would gain in the long run, and a well-aimed intervention can correct that. The second assumption is about politics. The society-centered approach treats national policy as the resultant of interest-group forces. The state-centered approach says that under certain institutional conditions, policymakers are insulated enough to pursue an objective of their own — boosting a strategic industry, catching a rival, securing a supply — that no domestic lobby necessarily demanded. The crucial logical point for students: you need both assumptions together. If protection can never raise welfare, then autonomy just lets the state destroy value more efficiently. If the state can never act autonomously, then even a real welfare opportunity gets captured and distorted by whoever lobbies hardest. The rest of the lecture is essentially an examination of when — and whether — these two strong assumptions actually hold.

Revisiting Core Assumptions - Market Failures Everywhere

Recall Day 3 (Ch. 3): the gains-from-trade verdict — do not intervene — rested on three assumptions we flagged as contested. The state-centered approach is favored by those who focus on the limits of those assumptions.

Assumption What Day 3 assumed How Ch. 5 relaxes it →
Market clearing Factors are allocated to their best use; no lasting unemployment — so a tariff can only destroy value. Scale + learning curves strand factors below their long-run value — a social-vs-private return gap the market won't close without public guidance.
Constant / decreasing returns Markets are competitive: many firms, no market power. High-tech runs on increasing returns and oligopoly. A few firms earn rents, and the country that hosts them captures the income.
Neutral terms of trade Doesn't really matter what goods you specialize in. National wealth accrues when countries can move up the value chain. Staying stuck in primary production is bad for wealth and growth.

Relax perfect competition and constant returns and the case for free trade becomes more complicated and less absolute.

This is a deliberate callback, and it’s worth making explicit because it shows students that Chapter 5 is not a new and unrelated topic — it is, in effect, the mirror image of the model we built in Chapter 3. Remember the “What This Theory Assumes” slide from Day 3: I flagged three assumptions sitting behind the clean gains-from-trade result, and I promised we would come back to them. Here we are. Take them in order. One, market clearing: Day 3 assumed factors glide to their highest-value use, which is precisely why a tariff could only ever block that movement and destroy value. The state-centered approach denies the premise — economies of scale and learning curves can strand resources below their long-run potential, opening a gap between social and private returns that the market won’t close on its own, and that gap is the entire welfare case for intervention we’re about to build. Two, constant or decreasing returns: the standard model assumed competition, many firms, nobody with market power. Drop that assumption and you land in the world of increasing returns and oligopoly, where a handful of firms earn rents and the country hosting them pockets the income. Notice this is the “new trade theory” referred to on the Day 3 slide — Krugman’s world, and the engine of strategic-trade theory in Section 3. Three, neutral terms of trade: relax the idea that no producer is systematically favored and you see that rents and first-mover advantages are contestable prizes a government can try to seize with a subsidy. So the throughline to carry for the rest of the hour is this: every tool we discuss today — infant-industry protection, strategic-trade subsidies, even the security measures at the end — lives in the space opened up by relaxing one or more of these three assumptions. That gives you a diagnostic you can use on any real-world intervention, including the chip subsidies in today’s article: ask which assumption the advocate is claiming fails here. If they can’t name one, they’re probably not doing Chapter 5 at all — they’re back in Chapter 4, doing interest-group politics with a national-interest vocabulary.

Infant Industries and Industrial Policy

The Infant-Industry Case

A new industry may be unprofitable today but efficient tomorrow — if it survives long enough to grow up. Two mechanisms create that gap:

Economies of scale

Big fixed costs (an aircraft program ≈ $3B+). Unit cost falls only at high volume. A new entrant can’t reach that volume against incumbents who already have.

Economies of experience

Efficiency comes from doing — seasoned managers, skilled workers, reliable suppliers. The learning curve accrues through cumulative production; it cannot simply be purchased.

Recall Mercantialism: Alexander Hamilton (1791) and Friedrich List both sought tariffs to develop manufacturing against a dominant Britain — and the same argument was later applied to postwar Japan.

The economic heart of the chapter is the infant-industry argument, and it is worth slowing down because every modern industrial-policy debate is a variation on it. The standard trade model rules out welfare-improving intervention by assumption: factors flow to their highest return, so a tariff only blocks that flow. To justify intervention you must identify something that stops factors from moving into an industry that would, in the long run, pay off. The classic answer is the infant industry — a firm not efficient initially but efficient eventually, if it is given time to mature. Two mechanisms generate that gap. Economies of scale: when fixed costs are enormous — Oatley’s example is a commercial aircraft program costing billions — unit costs fall only at high volume, but a newcomer can’t sell enough high-cost early output against established low-cost rivals to ever reach that volume. Economies of experience, or learning by doing: efficient production needs seasoned managers, skilled workers, and reliable suppliers, none of which exist in an infant industry by definition. Costs fall as you accumulate experience. The political pedigree matters for an IPE course: this is not a left-wing idea or a new idea. Alexander Hamilton made the argument in 1791 to build American manufacturing against British dominance; Friedrich List systematized it in Germany a half-century later; and many credit Japan’s postwar ascent to the same logic. Free-trade Britain was the incumbent; everyone climbing behind it reached for the tariff.

Climbing the Learning Curve

The newcomer’s dilemma: costs fall only with volume, but volume is unattainable while costs remain high. A temporary tariff or subsidy can solve this “low-level equilibrium trap” .

This figure makes the learning curve concrete and gives students something visual to remember. When Airbus assembled its very first fuselage it took roughly 340,000 person-hours. By the 75th aircraft that had fallen to about 85,000, and eventually to around 43,000. That improvement was not purchased; it was achieved through cumulative repetition — workers learning the job, managers refining the process, suppliers becoming reliable. Now see the trap. The newcomer is expensive precisely because it hasn’t yet produced much, and it can’t produce much because, being expensive, it can’t win customers away from incumbents who are already far down their own curve. Who buys the high-cost early output? In an open market, no one — so the firm never reaches the volume that would make it competitive. This is the welfare argument for a temporary intervention: guarantee the infant the domestic market for a while, let it produce enough to slide down the curve, and once it’s genuinely competitive, remove the protection. The phrase to underline is temporary. The entire welfare case depends on the tariff coming off once the industry grows up. Hold that thought, because the most serious real-world criticism of industrial policy is that the protection almost never comes off — the infant becomes a dependent adult with a powerful lobby. We’ll return to it.

A Tariff Is Rarely the Best Tool

The economists’ rule: target the actual distortion.

  1. A profitable-but-cash-starved firm should borrow — and if financing is the binding constraint, the government can subsidize the borrowing itself (a loan or guarantee), not the output.
  2. A skills-spillover problem needs workforce training, not a tariff.
  3. Intervention is justified only where a real market failure creates a major coordination problem (government needs to make both the demand and supply side of the market).

This slide is Oatley’s “A Closer Look” box, and it teaches students how an economist actually reasons about intervention — by asking what specific market failure you’re correcting and choosing the narrowest tool that fixes it. Start at the left. A tariff helps the infant, but it does so by taxing domestic consumers, who now pay more — a real welfare loss. So move right: a subsidy delivers the same help to the firm without gouging consumers, which is why economists prefer it to a tariff. But now comes the sharper objection, and it applies to subsidies too. If a firm really will be profitable in the long run, it should be able to borrow against those future profits in private capital markets to cover its early losses. If it can borrow, it doesn’t need the government. And if it can’t borrow — if private investors, looking at the same future, decline to fund it — then the market is telling you this isn’t society’s best use of scarce capital, so the government shouldn’t fund it either. That argument is the free-market case against industrial policy in compressed form. It fails in exactly two circumstances, and they’re worth memorizing because they define the legitimate zone for intervention. One: the payoff comes from training workers who can then quit and take their skills to rivals, so no single firm can capture the return — a classic spillover, best fixed by broad training programs, not firm subsidies. Two: the capital market itself is broken and won’t lend to good projects — in which case the first-best fix is to repair the capital market, not to subsidize the firm. The through-line: intervention is defensible only where you can name a real market failure that blocks the private borrowing solution. Keep that test in mind for the security debate later — “China might cut us off” is a different kind of market failure, and we’ll ask whether it passes.

State Strength: Who Can Actually Do This?

Industrial policy = tax breaks, subsidies and state credit, protection, and procurement, used to steer resources toward favored industries.

Whether a state can do so depends on state strength — how insulated policymakers are from interest groups.

State Institutions & track record Insulation
Japan — STRONG Centralized authority; MITI/METI + Ministry of Finance coordinate. "Administrative guidance" steered capital from heavy industry (1950s) to high-tech (1970s). High
France — STRONG Insulated bureaucracy; "National Champions" strategy. Backed Renault, steel — and a national computer firm (Machines Bull) that flopped. High
United States — WEAK Federalism + divided powers + porous agencies → captured easily. Yet runs a *de facto* industrial policy through DEFENSE: R&D, procurement, the internet, early chips. Low (but the Pentagon is a loophole)

Strength cuts both ways. Insulation lets a state plan for the long run and pull protection once the infant matures — but it also removes the check that stops the state from picking badly.

Now we connect the economics to institutions, because even if intervention can work in theory, only some states are equipped to implement it. First, the definition students need for the quiz: industrial policy is the use of a broad toolkit — tax policy, subsidies including state-directed credit, traditional protection, and government procurement — to channel resources away from some industries and toward those the state wants to grow. The capacity to wield that toolkit coherently is what Oatley calls state strength: the degree to which policymakers are insulated from interest-group pressure. Strong states centralize authority, coordinate across agencies, and offer few access points to lobbyists. Japan is the textbook strong state — MITI, now METI, working with the Ministry of Finance, used “administrative guidance” to move capital from steel and shipbuilding in the early postwar years into high-technology in the 1970s, and many credit this for the Japanese miracle. France is the other strong-state case, with its insulated bureaucracy and its “National Champions” strategy — though notice Oatley’s honest detail that the French national computer champion, Machines Bull, failed to become competitive, an early reminder that strength guarantees coherence, not success. The United States is the canonical weak state: federalism, separated powers, and porous agencies create so many channels for lobbying that officials struggle to act coherently in any “national interest.” And yet — this is the move I want students to catch — the U.S. runs a large de facto industrial policy through the back door of national defense: Pentagon R&D and procurement underwrote computing, the internet, and, as we’ll see in a moment, the early semiconductor industry. That defense loophole is the historical seed of today’s “economic security” turn. Last point, and it’s the double edge: insulation is exactly what lets a strong state plan long-term and, crucially, withdraw protection once an industry matures — but it’s also what removes the feedback that would tell the state it has chosen badly. Strength is not the same as good judgment.

Strategic-Trade Theory

When Markets Have Room for Only a Few

Strategic-trade theory (Krugman; Brander & Spencer 1985) extends the infant-industry logic to oligopoly — industries with room for only a handful of firms.

  • Big scale + steep learning ⇒ only 2–3 firms worldwide can survive (aircraft: ~2; autos: ~8)
  • These firms earn rents — profits above what the same capital and labor earn elsewhere
  • Whoever hosts the firm captures the rents as national income
  • So there’s a zero-sum race: only a few countries can win

Absent intervention, the first mover wins — early scale and experience let it deter every later entrant, even one that would have been just as good.

The core policy idea: a government can use a subsidy to shift the rents from a foreign firm to a domestic one — making its own country richer at the rival’s expense.

Strategic-trade theory is the intellectual high-water mark of the case for intervention, and it’s associated above all with Paul Krugman and with the 1985 Brander–Spencer model. It takes the infant-industry insight and drops it into a world of oligopoly. The setup: when economies of scale and experience are severe enough, the world market only has room for a handful of firms — two for long-haul commercial aircraft, about eight for autos. Because entry is so limited, these firms earn rents: returns above what the same labor and capital would earn in their next-best use. The nationalist implication is this: whichever country hosts the firm captures those rents as higher national income, in the form of better-paid workers and higher returns to investors. Since only a few firms can exist, only a few countries can capture the rents, which sets up a zero-sum competition among states over who gets the industry. Who wins, absent any government action? The first mover. By entering early and racing down the scale-and-experience curve, the incumbent builds a cost advantage that deters later entrants — even an entrant who, given the same head start, would have been every bit as competitive. That’s a crucial and somewhat unfair feature: the winner wins because it was first, not because it was better. And that opens the radical policy door. If a government can subsidize a domestic late-entrant enough to make entry credible, it can alter which firm survives — shifting the rents, and the national income, from the foreign incumbent to the home firm. This is rent-shifting, and it follows a genuinely beggar-thy-neighbor logic: one country’s gain is the other’s loss. Consider it as a game.

The Rent-Shifting Game

A subsidy of 25 relocates rents of 125 and induces the rival’s exit. This is the formal case that intervention can raise national income.

This is Krugman’s 1987 illustration, Oatley’s Figure 5.1, and it’s worth building live on the board if you have time. Two firms, one American and one European; the market supports only one producer. Each chooses “produce” or “don’t.” Look at the left matrix first. If only one produces, it earns 100 and the other earns 0 — those are the two equilibria, the top-right cell where the U.S. produces and Europe stays out, and the bottom-left where Europe produces and the U.S. stays out. If both produce, they split a market too small for two and both lose money, here minus 5 each. So the outcome is indeterminate in theory and settled in practice by who moves first: the incumbent produces, the entrant rationally stays out because entering only earns it a loss, and the incumbent keeps the 100. Now the right matrix. Suppose the European government commits a subsidy of 25 to its firm whenever it produces. Re-read Europe’s payoffs: if the U.S. produces, Europe now earns 20 instead of losing 5 by producing; if the U.S. stays out, Europe earns 125. Producing is now better for Europe no matter what the U.S. does — it’s a dominant strategy. The American firm, anticipating this, knows Europe will produce, so its best response is to exit. The single equilibrium flips: Europe captures the market and earns 125. Tally it for the European treasury: it spent 25 in subsidy to relocate 100 of rents — plus the extra 25 — onto its own soil. A small public outlay levers a large private rent away from the rival. That is the formal heart of strategic-trade theory, and one can see why it unsettled free-trade economists: here is a model in which a subsidy does not merely protect a domestic industry but displaces the foreign one. Now I want you holding two questions, because we’ll spend the back half of the lecture on them. First, does this actually work outside the diagram? Second — and notice the payoff structure already hints at it — what happens when the other government subsidizes as well? The result depends on the rival government remaining passive.

Rent-Shifting in the Wild: Chips and Jets

The U.S. built both industries first — through defense procurement. Japan (MITI) and Europe (Airbus) then used protection and subsidy to capture a share of the rents. This generates international political conflict over industrial policy. Previously, the US used the GATT/WTO largely to try to reverse other countries’ industrial policies. Now, US is increasingly taking a “if you can’t beat ’em, join ’em” attitude.

Strategic-trade theory isn’t only a diagram; Oatley gives two cases where the rent-shifting story fits the record reasonably well. Semiconductors first — and specifically DRAM chips, the segment where the data is cleanest. The U.S. got there first, and notice how — not through a deliberate industrial policy but through defense. Pentagon purchases absorbed up to 100 percent of early semiconductor output and still 40 percent into the late 1960s, which let American firms race down the learning curve. Then, from the 1970s, Japan’s MITI targeted the chip industry with exactly the strategic-trade toolkit: tariffs and quotas to keep American chips out, restrictions on foreign investment so U.S. firms couldn’t jump the barrier, procurement favoring Japanese chips, and R&D subsidies across dozens of projects. The result is the left panel: the U.S. share of global DRAM production collapsed from about 70 percent in 1978 to about 20 percent by 1986, while Japan’s rose from under 30 percent to about 75 percent over the same years. The rents moved. The right panel is the aircraft mirror image, and now plots both firms directly so the transfer is visible as two crossing lines, the same way the left panel shows it. Boeing and McDonnell Douglas dominated postwar, again partly on the back of military contracts — the 707 was derived from a military tanker. Then in 1967 France, Germany, and Britain launched Airbus and poured in somewhere between 10 and 18 billion dollars, about 75 percent of development cost. By 1990 Boeing held about 62 percent of large-aircraft deliveries — with McDonnell Douglas, not shown on this simplified chart, holding most of the rest — against Airbus’s roughly 15 percent; by 2003 Airbus had overtaken Boeing outright for the first time, 52 percent to 48 percent. A large slice of the industry’s rents transferred from the U.S. to Europe. Two honest caveats for students. First, these are the cases that fit; the failures — Machines Bull, Concorde, many MITI initiatives — are less often cited, and selecting on successes is exactly the trap we’ll name in the objections section. Second, look at what actually founded both industries: not visionary economic planning but defense spending. The state was already in these industries for security reasons before anyone articulated a competitiveness rationale — which is precisely the issue the modern debate reopens.

From Competitiveness to Security

Same Tools, New Objective

For two centuries the case for industrial policy was economic — growth, productivity, competitiveness, rents. Since roughly 2017 the dominant case has become strategic — supply-chain resilience, chokepoints, and not depending on a rival.

Here is the pivot the whole lecture has been building toward, and it’s the one I most want you to leave with. The toolkit of industrial policy — subsidies, procurement, protection, state credit — has barely changed in two hundred years. What has changed, and changed sharply in the last decade, is the justification. From Hamilton through postwar Japan through 1980s strategic-trade theory, the argument was fundamentally economic: build the industry because it raises growth, productivity, competitiveness, captures rents, employs people in high-wage jobs. The yardstick was national income. Read the Sullivan and policy language of the 2020s and the yardstick has moved. The CHIPS Act, the Inflation Reduction Act’s clean-energy provisions, the October 2022 export controls — these are defended primarily as security measures: keep critical supply chains resilient, deny a rival access to advanced technology, and don’t let the country be dependent on an adversary for things it cannot live without. The timeline color shift on the slide is the argument in one image — crimson competitiveness on the left bleeding into navy security on the right. Two cautions so we don’t caricature this. The shift is in emphasis, not kind; competitiveness arguments never vanished, and security arguments are old — recall that the U.S. semiconductor and aircraft industries were founded on defense money. And the dating is rough; you can put the inflection at the 2017 National Security Strategy, or the 2018 tariffs, or COVID’s supply-chain shock, or the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The point is not a precise date but a reframing: the same policy that an economist would judge by whether it raises national income is now judged by whether it keeps the nation safe. Those are different tests, they do not always agree, and much of today’s discussion depends on distinguishing them.

Why Policy Makers Now See Dependence as Vulnerability

Trade always created security externalities — but whether interdependence contributes to peace or vulnerability depends on the geopolitical environment.

Liberal Internationalism (1990s)

Interdependence as mutual restraint. Gains from trade bind rivals together. Depending on others is efficient.

Economic Security (now)

Networks have chokepoints. The state that controls a hub — the dollar, SWIFT, advanced chips — can deny a rival access or surveil the flows passing through it (Farrell & Newman 2019, weaponized interdependence).

Trade follows the flag. Free trade is more likely within alliances than across them — because trade with an ally strengthens you, trade with a rival strengthens them (Gowa & Mansfield 1993; Hirschman 1945).

It’s not just states. Carnegie & Gaikwad (2022) — Day 4’s article — find the same pattern at the individual level: ordinary citizens are more supportive of trade with allies than with geopolitical rivals, even holding the economic terms of the deal constant.

The same dependence — on foreign chips, e.g. — may be interpreted as efficient specialization among allies and as a strategic liability among rivals.

If the rationale has shifted from competitiveness to security, the obvious question is: why now? Why does relying on foreign suppliers, which the gains-from-trade logic of Chapter 3 celebrates as efficient specialization, suddenly look like a threat? The IPE answer is that trade has always carried security externalities — consequences for the balance of power, not just for income — and how heavily those weigh depends on the geopolitical environment. Bring in two literatures. First, Gowa and Mansfield’s 1993 work, building on Albert Hirschman back in 1945: trade isn’t politically neutral, because the gains from trade are also gains in national capability. Trade with an ally strengthens a partner whose strength helps you; trade with a rival strengthens an adversary whose strength threatens you. So free trade clusters within alliances — “trade follows the flag” — and states worry about relative gains, about who is strengthened more, especially across a rivalry. Second, Farrell and Newman’s 2019 concept of weaponized interdependence, which updates Hirschman for the network age. Modern economic networks — dollar clearing, the SWIFT messaging system, the chip supply chain — aren’t flat; they run through a few central hubs. Whoever controls a hub gets two powers: the chokepoint, the ability to deny a rival access, and the panopticon, the ability to see everything flowing through. Put the two ideas together and you get the modern anxiety in one line, the bottom of the slide: the very same dependence reads completely differently depending on who’s at the other end. Relying on Taiwanese or Dutch chips among allies looks like sensible specialization; relying on a strategic rival for the same input looks like granting them control of a chokepoint. Nothing about the economics changed — what changed is the assessment of intent and the structure of the network. That is why the security environment, not the trade theory, is the decisive factor in today’s policy. Point at the second callout and connect it back to Day 4: Carnegie and Gaikwad’s finding — that ordinary people, not just states, prefer trading with allies over rivals even holding economic terms fixed — is the individual-level mirror of the state-level “trade follows the flag” argument. It’s worth naming explicitly, since it previews exactly the question our presenter will test on Bown and Wang: is the geopolitics-shapes-preferences logic we saw at the mass public level in Chapter 4 also what’s driving elite policy here in Chapter 5?

The New Toolkit — and the New Vocabulary

Instrument What it does — and why
CHIPS & Science Act (2022) USD 52.7B for domestic semiconductors (USD 39B in subsidies, a 25% tax credit, plus R&D), with "guardrails" barring expansion in China.
Inflation Reduction Act (2022) Hundreds of billions in clean-energy subsidies with domestic-content rules — climate policy *and* competition with China rolled together.
Export controls (Oct. 2022 →) Bars sales of advanced chips and chip-making tools to China. The goal is not to capture rents but to deny a rival a capability.
"Small yard, high fence" Sullivan's framing (2023): restrictions stay narrow and security-focused; a "New Washington Consensus" replaces reflexive liberalization.

Note the shift in objective. Classic industrial policy seeks to build domestic winners; export controls seek to deny rivals a capability.

This is the concrete contemporary toolkit, and it’s worth being precise because the article our presenter covers lives inside it. The CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 put 52.7 billion dollars behind domestic semiconductor manufacturing — about 39 billion in direct subsidies, a 25 percent investment tax credit, and roughly 13 billion for R&D and workforce — with “guardrails” that bar recipients from expanding advanced manufacturing in China. The Inflation Reduction Act layered on hundreds of billions in clean-energy subsidies with domestic-content requirements, which is fascinating precisely because it fuses three rationales at once — climate, jobs, and competition with China — and different advocates sell it with whichever rationale suits them. Then, two months after CHIPS, the October 2022 export controls, which are a genuinely different instrument: they do not try to build an American chip champion, but to deny China access to advanced chips and the tools to make them. Jake Sullivan’s 2023 Brookings speech gave this whole posture its slogans — “small yard, high fence,” meaning the restrictions are supposed to be narrow and security-focused rather than a general blockade, and a “New Washington Consensus” to replace the reflexive free-trade consensus of the 1990s. I want students to catch the shift in objective at the bottom of the slide, because it is analytically important. Classic industrial policy is constructive — it tries to build winners, and you can at least argue it against a market-failure benchmark. Export controls are destructive — they try to deny a rival a capability — and that has almost no market-failure justification at all; it’s straightforwardly an exercise of power. When we get to the objections, keep asking which kind of policy we’re evaluating, because the build-a-winner kind and the deny-a-rival kind fail in different ways and for different reasons.

Can the Line Be Drawn?

The slippery-slope risk: once “security” considerations overriding the market, almost any industry can invoke it. A criterion that applies to everything constrains nothing — and protectionist interests have strong incentives to adopt the language of security.

This slide is the Big Question made operational, and it’s the one I’d most like to spend discussion time on. Lay industries along a spectrum. At the left, things that are genuinely just economic — consumer solar panels, where the case is competitiveness or climate, not survival. At the right, things almost everyone agrees are security — munitions, the rare-earth magnets that go into guided weapons, nuclear and weapons technology — where you would not want to depend on an adversary at any price. The analytical action is all in the shaded middle: EV batteries, critical minerals, advanced semiconductors. Chips are the perfect hard case because they are simultaneously a consumer good, the foundation of economic competitiveness, and a military necessity — the same fab makes the chip in your phone and the chip in a missile. So is a chip subsidy industrial policy or defense policy? The honest answer is that you often cannot cleanly separate them, and that ambiguity is not a footnote — it’s the central governance problem. Here is the slippery-slope concern, and it is why economists are wary. The market-failure discipline established earlier — name the distortion, use the narrowest tool — has real constraining force. “National security” has almost no bite as a limiting principle, because with enough imagination every industry can tell a security story: food security, energy security, even “freedom” of various supply chains. If everything can be labeled security, then security stops doing any analytical work, and the label becomes exactly what a protectionist lobby reaches for when the economic argument has failed. Recall the Chapter 4 logic: concentrated producers are superb at organizing. Provide such interests with a rationale that suspends ordinary cost-benefit scrutiny, and the risk becomes evident. And note the irony I flagged earlier: Korea’s heavy-and-chemical drive, the cleanest success story in the new evidence, was itself launched for security reasons — North Korean provocations and a U.S. troop drawdown — and it happened to also build competitiveness. So security and competitiveness rationales aren’t always rivals; sometimes they point the same way. The analytical skill we are building is distinguishing the case where they genuinely align from the case where “security” functions as a pretext.

The Political Economy Objections

Three Reasons Industrial Policy Often Fails

  1. Picking winners (the knowledge problem). Effective intervention requires identifying the right sector and calibrating the right subsidy. Governments are frequently poor at both, and misjudged targeting misallocates capital.
  2. Rent-seeking & capture (Krueger; Olson). Once subsidies are available, firms have incentives to compete for them through lobbying rather than innovation; political connections, not productivity, can determine who benefits.
  3. The exit problem. Protection meant to be temporary tends to persist, as protected firms become a standing constituency for its continuation.

The underlying difficulty: industrial policy is designed for an idealized government but implemented by the actual one — subject to capture, short time horizons, and difficulty reversing failed commitments.

Dani Rodrik (most pro-industrial policy elite economist): the decisive question is less whether a state can pick winners than whether it can let losers go.

Now the case for the prosecution, because a good IPE student should be able to argue both sides fully. Three classic objections. First, the knowledge problem, popularly “picking winners.” Even granting that intervention can work in theory, the government has to do two hard things: identify which sector actually offers the long-run gains, and calculate the right size of subsidy. Get the sector wrong or the dose wrong and scarce capital is committed to an unviable sector — and the track record includes Concorde and Machines Bull. Second, and politically deeper, rent-seeking and capture, the logic of Anne Krueger and Mancur Olson. The moment subsidies are available, you change what firms optimize for: effort shifts from making a better product to lobbying for a larger subsidy, and the firms that win are the ones best at politics, not the ones best at production. Industrial policy can quietly convert an economy’s most capable firms into its most effective lobbyists. Third, the commitment problem — the “temporary” protection that never sunsets. The entire welfare case rested on removing the tariff once the infant grew up, but a protected industry becomes a concentrated, organized constituency for its own continued protection, and democratic governments rarely have the political will to withdraw it. Taken together, these yield the claim in the callout, which I regard as the single most important sentence in the objections: industrial policy is usually designed for an idealized government — far-sighted, disinterested, technically brilliant — and then implemented by the actual government we have, which is lobby-captured, electorally myopic, and structurally reluctant to acknowledge a failed commitment. Rodrik’s reframing is the constructive version and worth giving students: stop asking whether the state can pick winners, which demands near-omniscience, and ask the more achievable thing — can it let losers go? A system with hard budget constraints and real exit can tolerate many poor choices; a system that cannot terminate its failures will accumulate losses regardless of how well it selects. That’s also a usable evaluation rubric for the CHIPS-era programs: not “did they choose chips correctly” but “is there any mechanism to stop if it isn’t working?”

The Theory Suggests Targeted Interventions

The strategic-trade case for subsidies is not only hard to implement but theoretically limited.

Change one assumption… …and the optimal policy reverses
Firms compete on QUANTITY (Cournot) Brander & Spencer (1985): SUBSIDIZE your firm Subsidy shifts rents home
Firms compete on PRICE (Bertrand) Eaton & Grossman (1986): TAX your firm Subsidy is counterproductive; the opposite policy is optimal

Why the reversal? Cournot (quantity) firms treat output as a strategic substitute — a subsidy commits your firm to more, so the rival cuts back. Bertrand (price) firms treat price as a strategic complement — subsidizing triggers a mutual price war instead, so the fix is a tax on your own firm (not a tariff on the rival), committing it to a higher price the rival then matches. An assumption this subtle can flip the answer — proceed only where the failure and payoff are both large.

Subsidy wars happen — see EVs. China subsidized EV/battery champions for years (CATL alone: ~$77M in 2018 → ~$809M in 2023), building ~70% of world EV output and millions of surplus vehicles. The IRA (2022) answered with U.S. subsidies; 2024 brought 100% U.S. tariffs on Chinese EVs, 17–38% EU tariffs, and a matching 100% Canadian tariff. Rents dissipated across three governments at once.

The objections so far are about implementation, but there’s a deeper problem: the theory that licenses the subsidy is itself brittle, and this is the critique that made even Krugman cautious about his own creation. Brander and Spencer’s 1985 result — subsidize your national champion to shift rents home — depends on a specific assumption about how the firms compete, namely Cournot competition, where firms choose quantities. Take a beat to explain the mechanism, because “subsidize under quantity competition, tax under price competition” sounds arbitrary until you see why. Under Cournot, quantities are strategic substitutes: each firm’s best response to a bigger rival is to produce less. A subsidy lowers your firm’s cost, so it wants to produce more — and because your rival optimally scales back when you scale up, your firm’s output gain comes partly at the rival’s expense. The subsidy works like a commitment device, turning your firm into a Stackelberg leader. Eaton and Grossman showed in 1986 that if you instead assume Bertrand competition — firms choosing prices on differentiated products — the strategic logic flips, because prices are strategic complements: rivals tend to raise or lower prices together, not in opposite directions. Under Bertrand, a subsidy would push your firm to cut its price to grab share, but since prices move together, the rival cuts its price too, and you get a mutual price war that burns profits on both sides — the subsidy backfires. The government’s best move is the opposite: tax your own firm’s output. That’s the point most likely to confuse students, so say it explicitly — this is not a tariff on the foreign rival’s imports, it’s a tax on your own exporter, and its purpose is to credibly commit your firm to a higher price. Because prices are strategic complements, the rival responds by raising its price too, competition softens, and your firm’s smaller-but-higher-margin sales beat what an all-out price war would have delivered. Consider how damaging this reversal is for policymaking: the very same model, the same firms, the same rents — and flipping one assumption that is essentially invisible to a policymaker flips the recommendation from “subsidize” to “tax,” from help to hurt. Brander and Spencer offered an example, not a general law. And the fragility compounds: in general equilibrium, subsidizing the strategic sector pulls resources out of other industries, taxing them implicitly; and the whole result assumes the rival government sits still, when in reality it often doesn’t. Point to the right-hand callout for the real-world version of that failure, and use a different case than the Boeing-Airbus one from two slides ago so it doesn’t feel repetitive: electric vehicles and batteries. China spent well over a decade subsidizing its EV and battery champions — CATL’s disclosed subsidies alone rose from roughly $77 million in 2018 to roughly $809 million in 2023 — and the result is China now produces something like 70 percent of the world’s EVs, with estimates of several million to as many as twenty million vehicles of surplus capacity. Rather than sit still, the U.S. answered with its own production subsidies and tightened content rules under the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act, and in 2024 both the U.S. and the EU moved to tariffs — 100 percent on Chinese EVs from the U.S., 17 to 38 percent from the EU depending on the manufacturer, and Canada matched the U.S. at 100 percent. Three governments, three rounds of subsidies, and now three sets of tariffs — precisely the rent-dissipation outcome the theory warns about when the rival doesn’t stay passive, playing out in real time in an industry this class already knows from the IRA discussion two slides back. So the honest verdict on strategic-trade theory is the one Oatley reaches: it does not give robust, usable support for the claim that intervention reliably raises national income. It tells you intervention could help under precise conditions you usually can’t verify, with tools whose correct sign you can’t determine, against a rival that does not remain passive. It functions less as a policy prescription than as a caution.

Does Industrial Policy Work? Recent Evidence

Recent causal studies (Juhász, Lane & Rodrik 2024) reach more favorable conclusions than the earlier, largely skeptical literature — though their findings are strongly conditional.

Evidence it can work

  • South Korea’s HCI drive (1973–79): Lane shows it durably shifted comparative advantage into heavy industry — benefits spread downstream and persisted after the policy ended.
  • Juhász (Napoleonic blockade), Hanlon, and others find real, lasting effects in well-identified cases.

…but read the fine print

  • These are successes studied carefully, not a promise of replication.
  • Korea’s HCI was security-driven (North Korea, U.S. troop drawdown) — and still cost-laden and contested.
  • Evidence of success in one case does not establish transferability; context, state capacity, and exit discipline are decisive.

The position emerging from this literature is conditional rather than categorical: industrial policy can succeed under conditions that must be specified. The debate has moved from whether it works to when and how.

I don’t want to leave students with a caricature in either direction, because the actual research frontier is more interesting than “industrial policy good” or “industrial policy bad.” For decades the empirical literature was mostly skeptical, leaning on correlational studies and cautionary tales. The recent wave — Juhász, Lane, and Rodrik survey it in their 2024 Annual Review piece, “The New Economics of Industrial Policy” — uses modern causal-inference tools and reaches a more positive, though carefully hedged, conclusion. The flagship case is Nathan Lane’s study of South Korea’s Heavy and Chemical Industry drive from 1973 to 1979. Using the input-output structure of the economy, Lane shows the policy genuinely shifted Korea’s comparative advantage into targeted heavy industries, that the gains propagated to downstream users of those intermediates, and — the striking part — that the benefits persisted even after the program ended. Juhász’s work on the Napoleonic blockade as a natural experiment in protection, and several other well-identified studies, point the same way: in specific cases, well-designed promotion left a lasting mark. But teach the fine print as carefully as the headline. First, these are successes examined closely; they do not certify that the next program will work — there is an obvious selection issue in which episodes receive careful econometric treatment. Second, look again at Korea: the HCI drive was launched for national-security reasons, after North Korean provocations and a U.S. troop drawdown, which is a notable illustration that the security and competitiveness rationales we tried to separate were fused in the very case that most strongly supports industrial policy. And it was still enormously costly and remains contested. So the mature position, the one I want you to be able to defend, is neither the free-marketeer’s “never” nor the planner’s “always.” It’s “sometimes, under conditions you have to specify” — state capacity to choose and to exit, a real market failure or security rationale, and discipline to stop. The field has moved from arguing about whether industrial policy works to arguing about when and how — which is exactly the question contemporary semiconductor policy is now testing.

Weaknesses + Handoff

Oatley’s Verdict: A Fatal Flaw?

  1. No microfoundations. The model asserts states act for national welfare. But why would autonomous officials enrich society rather than themselves? (Think Versailles.) What’s the reward structure?
  2. Autonomy is overstated. Even Japan’s MITI served the LDP’s big-business base. “Autonomous” states still answer to some coalition.
  3. Strategic-trade theory is weak. As shown above: sensitive to assumptions, hard to target, and reversible in sign.

Oatley’s view: the missing microfoundations are a fatal flaw. The approach tells us autonomous states will act — but not how they’ll act, or why toward welfare.

The fix may be to combine approaches: states with real autonomy, but acting within limits set by the coalition that keeps them in power.

Before the handoff, Oatley’s own balance sheet, because he is notably hard on the approach he’s just taught. Three weaknesses. First and most fundamental, the lack of microfoundations. The state-centered approach asserts that autonomous states act to raise national welfare, but it never explains why they would. An autonomous ruler — picture Versailles — has at least as much incentive to enrich himself and his circle as to enrich society. So what reward structure makes individual officials choose national welfare? It can’t be elections, because that collapses you back into the society-centered model. It might be security competition — states that build strong economies survive — but nobody has worked out how that systemic pressure translates into the specific resource-allocation choices of particular bureaucrats. Oatley considers this gap serious enough to call it a fatal flaw: the approach can tell us an autonomous state will act, but not how it will act or why toward welfare rather than self-dealing. Second, the autonomy assumption is overstated in the other direction. Even Japan, the strongest of strong states, had a MITI whose industrial policy conspicuously favored big business — which happened to be the LDP’s political base. Was that the national interest, or the ruling coalition’s interest presented as the national interest? Even insulated states answer to someone. Third, the theoretical engine, strategic-trade theory, is weak in the ways we just dissected. Oatley’s constructive suggestion, and it’s the right note to end the analysis on, is synthesis: don’t choose between Chapters 4 and 5, combine them. Expect states to have genuine autonomy and objectives of their own, but exercised within boundaries set by the societal coalition that keeps them in power. That hybrid — autonomous within limits — is closer to how trade politics actually works than either pure model, and it’s the lens I’d carry into the article.
