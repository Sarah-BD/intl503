Course Format

This is an intensive four-week summer course. We meet Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday. Wednesdays are non-meeting reading days for catching up, working on quizzes, and office hours. The course is open to both advanced undergraduates and graduate students.

Course Description

This intensive four-week course provides a comprehensive introduction to international political economy (IPE). You’ll explore how states, firms, and international organizations shape global trade, investment, and monetary relations. We’ll examine major theoretical approaches and apply them to contemporary challenges including:

  • Trade conflicts, economic coercion, and protectionism
  • Development strategies and their political underpinnings
  • Multinational corporations and foreign direct investment
  • International financial crises and monetary cooperation
  • The future of global capitalism amid increased economic nationalism

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you’ll be able to analyze complex international economic issues through both society-centered and state-centered lenses, understand the politics behind economic policies, and engage critically with debates about globalization and its consequences. Specifically, you will be able to:

  1. Analyze international economic issues through both society-centered and state-centered theoretical lenses
  2. Identify the political determinants of international economic policies and outcomes
  3. Evaluate the evolution and effectiveness of international economic institutions
  4. Apply IPE theories to contemporary policy debates about trade, development, and finance
  5. Engage critically with debates about globalization, economic integration, and their consequences
  6. Read and evaluate emerging scholarship grappling with major changes in the global political economy

Required Text

Oatley, Thomas. International Political Economy. 7th edition. Routledge, 2023. ISBN: 9781032232669

The textbook is available through the IU Library, campus bookstore, and online retailers. The Oatley chapter is Reading 1 for each class session; we cover one chapter per session, with Chapters 6 and 7 paired into a single combined session.

All scholarly articles assigned as second readings will be posted to Canvas as PDFs (or linked through the IU Library) by the start of the term. Podcast episodes are linked directly on the Schedule page; no separate posting to Canvas is needed.

Course Structure and Expectations

Reading Load

Each class meeting has three assigned materials:

  1. A chapter from Oatley (the course textbook) — roughly 25–30 pages, providing the conceptual and theoretical foundation for the day.
  2. A scholarly article — typically a peer-reviewed journal article or book chapter that applies, complicates, or extends the chapter’s argument with contemporary evidence or a current policy case.
  3. A podcast episode — typically from Trade Talks (Soumaya Keynes & Chad Bown), with occasional episodes from other shows (Ideas of India, Planet Money/The Indicator, Foreign Correspondent, The Sanctions Age). A short, accessible companion that surfaces contemporary policy debates connected to the day’s material.

Plan for roughly 40–55 pages of reading plus one podcast episode (15–40 minutes) per class day. You should complete all assigned materials before the class session in which they are discussed. Wednesdays are non-meeting reading days that provide time to catch up if you fall behind.

Each student will sign up on Day 1 to present and lead discussion on one of the scholarly articles (see Article Presentation below). Note that Day 1 (July 6) has no scholarly article — that session is reserved for course logistics and a discussion of how to read political-economy scholarship — but it does have an assigned podcast episode. I will model the article presentation on Day 2 so you have a clear example before signing up for your own slot.

Podcast episode links for each day are posted on the Schedule page alongside that day’s chapter and article.

Class Sessions (2 hours, 10 minutes)

Each session typically includes:

  • Lecture and concept review on the day’s chapter (about 75 minutes)
  • A student-led article presentation and discussion (about 20 minutes; not every day)
  • Either an in-class writing activity and debrief (Mondays, Tuesdays, and Thursdays) or a quiz (Fridays of weeks 1, 2, and 3) — about 30 minutes

Together these run about 125 minutes, inside our 130-minute block. Active participation is essential in this format. Come prepared to discuss the assigned readings and engage with case examples.

Assignment Details

Quizzes (30% — 10% each)

Three quizzes given at the end of class on the Fridays of weeks 1, 2, and 3 — July 10, July 17, and July 24. Each quiz covers material from that week (chapters and articles). Quizzes consist of multiple-choice and short-answer questions and serve as comprehension checks that build toward the final exam.

Final Exam (25%)

Date: Friday, July 31, in class. The final is cumulative. It includes multiple-choice questions, short-answer questions on concept application, and an applied question grounded in a contemporary policy scenario.

Participation & Engagement (20% — 5% per week)

Active engagement is essential in an intensive online format. Participation is evaluated weekly and includes:

  • Attending class sessions prepared to discuss the assigned readings
  • Contributing thoughtful comments and questions during lecture and discussion
  • Engaging substantively with peers’ article presentations
  • Participating in small-group exercises and chat-based activities

Given the compressed format, missing even one class represents significant lost participation.

In-Class Writing Activities (15%)

Each Monday, Tuesday, and Thursday includes a short (~10-minute) in-class writing activity tied to the day’s material. Timing varies — sometimes the activity opens class to focus the reading, sometimes it comes toward the end of class as a synthesis check on a core concept from that day’s lecture. Prompts may ask you to summarize a key argument, apply a concept to a contemporary case, or stake out a position on a debate from the chapter.

Activities are graded credit / no-credit on a √, √+, √– scale:

  • √+ — Engages substantively with the material and presents a clear, supported response
  • √ — Completes the activity with evidence of having done the reading
  • √– — Submitted but missing key elements or showing limited engagement

Activities are not accepted late and cannot be made up except in cases of documented absence cleared in advance.

Article Presentation & Discussion (10%)

Most class days include one scholarly article (Reading 2) on a contemporary issue. Day 1 (July 6) is the exception — that session is reserved for course logistics and orientation. Each student will sign up on Day 1 to present one article and lead a 15–20 minute discussion of it. I will model the first presentation on Day 2 (July 7) so you have a clear example before your slot comes up.

Your presentation should:

  • Briefly summarize the article’s argument, evidence, and conclusion (5 minutes)
  • Connect the article to the day’s textbook chapter and broader course themes
  • Pose 2–3 discussion questions to the class
  • Facilitate the resulting discussion

Evaluation considers preparation, clarity, depth of engagement with the reading, and quality of discussion facilitation.

Course Policies

Attendance

Given the compressed format, missing one class session represents a full week of a regular semester course. More than one unexcused absence will negatively impact your grade. If you must miss class due to illness or emergency, contact me as soon as possible.

Late Work

In-class writing prompts cannot be made up. Quizzes and the final exam can only be rescheduled with advance notice and a documented, legitimate reason.

Academic Integrity

All work must be your own. Plagiarism or cheating will result in failure of the assignment and possible course failure. Consult IU’s academic integrity policies if you have questions about proper citation or collaboration.

Generative AI Policy

Generative AI tools may not be used during in-class writing prompts, quizzes, or the final exam. You may use AI tools to help you understand assigned readings outside of class — but you are responsible for verifying everything an AI tells you against the actual readings, and you remain responsible for your own learning.

Accessibility

If you need accommodations due to a disability, please contact me and Disability Services for Students as early as possible. I am committed to making this course accessible to all students.

Wellness

This is an intensive course during summer. Take care of your mental and physical health. If you’re struggling, please reach out — I’m happy to work with you to find solutions.
