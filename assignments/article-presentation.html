Article Presentation & Discussion

Lead the class through one scholarly article

Overview

Each student signs up on Day 1 (Monday, July 6) to present and lead discussion on one scholarly article over the course of the term. The presentation counts for 10% of your final grade.

Most class days have one supplementary scholarly article assigned alongside the textbook chapter — typically a journal article, working paper, or book chapter that engages a contemporary issue connected to that day’s reading. Day 1 (July 6) is the exception — that session is reserved for course logistics and orientation. I will model the first article presentation on Day 2 (July 7) with the Rickard (2024) article so you have a clear example before you present.

Sign-up

A sign-up sheet will be distributed at the start of Day 1. You’ll see the full list of articles for the term and pick one based on a topic that interests you. Sign-ups are first-come, first-served. Each article gets exactly one presenter.

Format (15–20 minutes total)

Brief summary (about 5 minutes). Walk the class through the article’s argument: what is the author claiming, what evidence are they bringing to bear, and what is their conclusion? Resist the temptation to summarize section-by-section — instead, identify the article’s core move and walk us through it.

Connection to course (about 3 minutes). How does this article relate to the day’s textbook chapter? Does it extend, complicate, or challenge what Oatley says? Where does it sit in the broader theoretical landscape we’ve been mapping?

Discussion questions (about 2 minutes). Pose 2–3 substantive discussion questions to the class. Strong discussion questions are open-ended (not yes/no), tied to specific passages or claims in the article, and connected to course themes.

Discussion facilitation (about 5–8 minutes). Lead the class through your discussion questions. You’re not lecturing — you’re calling on classmates, following up on their responses, drawing connections, and keeping the conversation moving.

Preparing well

The best presentations come from presenters who have:

  • Read the article at least twice — once to grasp the argument, once to engage critically
  • Identified the one or two most important claims, rather than trying to cover everything
  • Practiced the summary out loud so it fits within five minutes
  • Prepared discussion questions that they find genuinely interesting and that connect to active debates in the course

Evaluation criteria

Criterion What I’m looking for
Comprehension Clear, accurate grasp of the article’s argument and evidence
Integration Thoughtful connection to the course chapter and broader themes
Discussion design Substantive, open-ended questions tied to the article
Facilitation Effective leadership of the class conversation; responsive to peers
Time management Stays within the 15–20 minute window

Logistics

  • You need simple slides to overview the main ideas in the article. Simplicity is key: it is your job is to facilitate, not lecture.
  • Submit your discussion questions to me via email by 8:00 AM the day of your presentation so I can include them in the day’s materials.
  • Plan to attend office hours the week before your presentation if you’d like feedback on your discussion questions.
